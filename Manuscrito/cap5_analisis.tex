\chapter{Discussion and Analysis}
\label{ch:analysis}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reported the quantitative evidence produced by the experimental campaign without offering any interpretation. The present chapter complements that material by examining the structural reasons behind the observed behaviour. Section~\ref{sec:an:models} compares the proposed covariance estimators and mixture-of-Gaussian variants, identifying the volume--shape trade-off that explains their relative ordering. Section~\ref{sec:an:reranking} assesses the generative--discriminative re-ranking strategies, with particular emphasis on the long-horizon Joint benchmark as the diagnostic regime that most clearly separates the useful from the brittle ones. Section~\ref{sec:an:backbone} examines the dependence of the framework on the choice of feature extractor. Section~\ref{sec:an:sample-eff} analyses the sample efficiency of the closed-form estimators as new class data accumulates. Section~\ref{sec:an:embedding} consolidates the statistical characterisation of the embedding space, covering PCA structure, inter-class separability, Gaussianity validation and probabilistic calibration. Section~\ref{sec:an:other} discusses computational footprint and limitations. Section~\ref{sec:an:summary} synthesises the principal findings.

\section{Comparative Analysis of Covariance Estimators}
\label{sec:an:models}

This section uses the principal results of Tables~\ref{tab:combined_benchmarks} and~\ref{tab:split_joint} to compare the proposed covariance estimators (Diag, Full, Hybrid, OAS, Ledoit--Wolf, Shared) and the mixture-of-Gaussian variants. The unifying principle that organises the discussion is the volume--shape decomposition of the multivariate Gaussian log-likelihood, made explicit in Equation~(4) of the methodology chapter: the quadratic Mahalanobis term encodes the \emph{shape} of the class manifold while the log-determinant encodes its \emph{volume}. The way an estimator handles each of these two terms determines its behaviour in practice.

\subsection{The Volume--Shape Trade-off}
\label{sec:an:vs}

Table~\ref{tab:combined_benchmarks} reveals a nuanced ordering across the benchmarks rather than a single universal hierarchy. On CIFAR-100, the expected ranking Diag~$<$~Full~$\le$~Hybrid holds precisely ($86.1\,\%$, $88.5\,\%$, $88.5\,\%$ respectively). A noteworthy deviation occurs on ImageNet-R, where Diag-Cov ($77.1\,\%$) outperforms both Full-Cov ($75.1\,\%$) and Hybrid-Cov ($75.1\,\%$): the inflated log-determinant of the rank-deficient full-rank estimator already penalises performance at the moderate per-class budget of ImageNet-R ($\overline{n_c}\!\approx\!150$), while the diagonal estimator's inherently stable volume term provides a natural advantage in this regime. On Caltech-256, Hybrid substantially outperforms Full by more than four accuracy points ($94.4\,\%$ versus $90.0\,\%$), with a further important exception: Full-Cov and Hybrid-Cov both fall at or below Diag-Cov ($90.1\,\%$ and $94.4\,\%$ versus $90.1\,\%$) unless the Hybrid's diagonal log-determinant suppresses the volume blow-up. The driver of this pattern is the volume term: whenever the empirical covariance has very small eigenvalues---which is the case on Caltech-256 and ImageNet-R because $n_c \ll d$---the log-determinant becomes the dominant component of the score and biases the decision towards classes with low-volume distributions, regardless of the actual quality of the fit. The Hybrid estimator decouples this effect by using the full inverse for the quadratic term (thereby preserving the discriminative power of the Mahalanobis distance) while computing the log-determinant on the diagonal of the covariance only (thereby suppressing the volume blow-up). The resulting estimator inherits the shape sensitivity of the full-rank Mahalanobis distance and the numerical stability of the diagonal log-determinant; it is the structural reason for its consistent advantage on the heavy-tailed benchmarks Caltech-256 and Joint (Table~\ref{tab:split_joint}).

\subsection{Linear Shrinkage in the Classification Regime}
\label{sec:an:shrinkage}

The Oracle Approximating Shrinkage (OAS) and Ledoit--Wolf estimators are designed to regularise the sample covariance by pulling it towards a structured target~\cite{Ledoit2004,Chen2010}. Empirically, however, they recover the unrestricted accuracy only on CIFAR-100, where shrinkage is barely needed, and they lose 2--4 accuracy points on the remaining benchmarks. The explanation is again rooted in the volume term: by pulling the eigenvalues towards the trace average, linear shrinkage flattens the spectrum and removes precisely the volume contrast that the Bayesian classifier exploits to discriminate between classes with similar centroids. The conceptual point is that shrinkage repairs the covariance \emph{for estimation purposes}, but the downstream task is classification, where the relevant quantity is the ratio of likelihoods across classes rather than the absolute likelihood of any single class. The two shrinkage estimators are therefore best regarded as baselines for the more nuanced Hybrid estimator and not as competitive solutions on their own.

\subsection{Mixture Models and the Multi-Modality Question}
\label{sec:an:mog}

The MoG variants MOG2-D/F/H and MOG4-D/F/H assess whether modelling multi-modal class-conditional distributions can yield additional gains. The empirical pattern is uneven. Mixture models slightly outperform a single Gaussian on Caltech-256---where intra-class variability is sufficient to justify two or four components---but offer at best a draw on CIFAR-100, where the class-conditional features are already well modelled by a single Gaussian. The most striking observation is the collapse of MOG4-F and MOG4-H on the Joint benchmark, where accuracy drops from approximately 80\,\% down to 59\,\% (Table~\ref{tab:split_joint}). Two compounding factors explain this collapse: the parameter budget of four full-covariance components per class in $d=768$ amounts to $1.18\times 10^{6}$ free parameters, an order of magnitude beyond the per-class sample budget inherited from Caltech-256; and the BIC selector embedded in the implementation occasionally favours a sub-optimal number of components when the underlying density is unimodal but rotated. The practical recommendation is therefore to deploy mixtures selectively, in settings where the class density is genuinely multi-modal, and to avoid them otherwise.

\subsection{Positioning Against Prompt-Based Baselines}
\label{sec:an:positioning}

The headline numbers of Table~\ref{tab:combined_benchmarks} place the proposed framework within one accuracy point of the strongest prompt-based competitor (RainbowPrompt, $89.86\,\%$) on CIFAR-100, within two points of APT ($79.40\,\%$) on ImageNet-R, and ahead of every prompt-based method on Caltech-256 ($94.4\,\%$ versus $92.5\,\%$). The forgetting columns are equally informative: on the 10-task benchmarks the framework remains below five percentage points despite using no rehearsal mechanism. This behaviour is the structural consequence of the design choice: class statistics are estimated independently and frozen at the moment of insertion, so degradation of past classes cannot occur in the conventional sense. The forgetting values observed in practice therefore reflect the increased difficulty of the multi-class decision problem as the number of competing prototypes grows---the same phenomenon diagnosed quantitatively by the decay of the minimum pairwise KL divergence in Figure~\ref{fig:kl_divergence}---and not the loss of previously stored knowledge.

\section{Evaluation of the Generative--Discriminative Re-ranking Family}
\label{sec:an:reranking}

The methodology introduces ten re-ranking strategies (HardGate, Top5-TaskRR, TopK-TaskFilt, SoftTaskFus, TaskMeanShr, HardGate-LDA, SoftGate-LDA, Top5-CosRR, OptFusion-MM and TempScale) that operate on top of the Bayesian classifier. Their utility is not uniform: it depends on the underlying benchmark, on whether the re-ranker is paired with the diagonal or full-covariance branch, and on whether the auxiliary task-level distribution is itself well calibrated. Tables~\ref{tab:hybrid_cifar}--\ref{tab:hybrid_joint} provide the empirical basis for the analysis that follows.

\subsection{The Limits of Hard Task Gating}
\label{sec:an:hardgate}

HardGate, Top5-TaskRR and TopK-TaskFilt, together with their LDA counterparts HardGate-LDA and SoftGate-LDA, rely on a single global Gaussian per task to either restrict the candidate set or to project features into a task-specific subspace. On CIFAR-100 (Table~\ref{tab:hybrid_cifar}) all four strategies underperform the unrestricted Bayesian classifier when paired with the diagonal branch, by margins of 3--13 accuracy points. The cause is structural: when the diagonal Gaussian is already well calibrated, the additional volume contribution of a task-level prior introduces noise rather than information. The full-covariance branch is more forgiving (the volume term carries more information there) but still does not benefit from hard gating: TopK-TaskFilt attains $88.5\,\%$, identical to the unrestricted Full-Cov baseline. The conclusion is that hard task gating is brittle on the in-distribution benchmark and should be reserved for settings in which the task identity can be recovered with high confidence---a condition that rarely holds in class-incremental learning.

\subsection{Robustness of Additive Log-Domain Fusion}
\label{sec:an:soft}

The two soft strategies SoftTaskFus and TaskMeanShr exploit the same task-level information as the hard variants but combine it additively in the log-domain. On every benchmark and every covariance branch they outperform the corresponding hard variants by 5--15 accuracy points, and on Caltech-256 they achieve the highest non-prompt accuracy reported in this thesis ($95.0\,\%$ with Full-Cov plus SoftTaskFus). The robustness of additive fusion is structural: the task likelihood acts as a Bayesian prior that reinforces coherent decisions but cannot veto a strong class-level signal. This behaviour is a textbook example of well-behaved hierarchical evidence aggregation and provides the cleanest illustration in this work of the principle that hierarchical evidence should be combined multiplicatively (i.e.\ additively in the log-domain) rather than gated discretely.

\subsection{Discriminative Refinement via Cosine and Min--Max Fusion}
\label{sec:an:cosine}

Top5-CosRR and OptFusion-MM combine the Mahalanobis log-likelihood with the angular similarity between the input feature and the class centroid. They are consistently competitive: OptFusion-MM (the Optuna-tuned min--max fusion) attains the best $A_{10}$ on three of the four benchmarks (CIFAR-100, Caltech-256 and Joint with Full-Cov). The mechanism is geometric: as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:an:spectrum} below, DINOv2 produces features that are approximately isotropic at the global scale, so the cosine similarity captures directional information that the absolute log-likelihood discards (the unit-norm direction of the feature vector). The two scores are therefore complementary, and the Optuna search converges to fusion weights $\alpha\!\in\![0.30, 0.85]$ depending on the benchmark, with the cosine term contributing most on the heavy-tailed benchmarks and the log-likelihood term dominating on CIFAR-100. The simpler ``re-rank the Top-5 candidates by cosine'' variant (Top5-CosRR) is a strong runner-up and exposes no hyperparameter, which makes it an attractive default whenever a hyperparameter search is impractical.

\subsection{Temperature Scaling as Posterior Smoothing}
\label{sec:an:tempscale}

The TempScale strategy (Section~3.6 of the methodology, denoted MahalanobisT) does not target accuracy but the conjoint goals of low forgetting and good calibration. Across the four benchmarks it produces the lowest forgetting in the Full-Cov branch on CIFAR-100 ($4.05\,\%$) and is consistently among the three best in $F_{10}$. The temperature parameter $T$ acts on the log-likelihood by contracting the log-gap between the top-1 and top-2 candidates, with the dual effect of stabilising the maximum-a-posteriori decision as classes accumulate and of reducing the ECE, as confirmed by the additional metrics in Section~\ref{sec:an:calibration}. Like OptFusion-MM, TempScale benefits from per-dataset tuning via Optuna; the optimal $T^{*}$ ranges from $0.02$ on CIFAR-100 (where the unscaled log-gaps are already moderate) to $5.7$ on ImageNet-R (where the log-gaps grow by several orders of magnitude).

\subsection{The Joint Benchmark as a Diagnostic Lens}
\label{sec:an:joint}

Table~\ref{tab:hybrid_joint} is the most informative single result table in this thesis, because the long-horizon Joint stream amplifies the differences between strategies that would otherwise saturate on the 10-task benchmarks. The unrestricted Diag-Cov classifier reaches $A_{30}=78.32\,\%$ and the unrestricted Full-Cov reaches $A_{30}=78.65\,\%$. However, the Full-Cov branch \emph{collapses} to 47--53\,\% under HardGate, Top5-TaskRR, TopK-TaskFilt, SoftTaskFus and TaskMeanShr, while the same strategies stay at 78--79\,\% in the Diag-Cov branch. The mechanism is the determinant ill-conditioning of the high-dimensional Full-Cov estimator (detailed in Section~\ref{sec:an:gauss} below): when the task-level Gaussian inherits the same rank deficiency, hard gating amplifies its mis-routing errors dramatically. The single Full-Cov re-ranker that survives is OptFusion-MM, which attains $A_{30}=81.94\,\%$ and improves the unfused classifier by $3.3$ accuracy points---the largest gain produced by any re-ranker on any benchmark in this thesis. TempScale recovers $A_{30}=69.7\,\%$, an 18-point recovery over the worst hard-gating result, without any additional tuning beyond the Optuna search of $T$. These two strategies therefore play the dual role of accuracy enhancer (OptFusion-MM) and robustness mechanism (TempScale).

\subsection{Summary Taxonomy of Re-ranking Strategies}
\label{sec:an:taxonomy}

Aggregating the evidence above, the ten re-ranking strategies fall into three clearly distinguishable groups:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Recommended strategies:} SoftTaskFus, TaskMeanShr, Top5-CosRR, OptFusion-MM and TempScale. These rarely degrade the unrestricted baseline and frequently improve it, with absolute gains of up to $3.3$ points on the Joint stream.
	\item \textbf{Brittle strategies:} HardGate, Top5-TaskRR and TopK-TaskFilt. These restrict the candidate set on the basis of a single Gaussian decision and amplify task-mis-routing errors; they should be reserved for scenarios with a small number of well-separated tasks.
	\item \textbf{Marginal strategies:} HardGate-LDA and SoftGate-LDA. The added LDA subspace projection improves their robustness slightly relative to their non-LDA counterparts, but they do not beat the simpler additive-fusion variants and entail a higher computational cost.
\end{itemize}

\section{Sensitivity of the Framework to the Choice of Backbone}
\label{sec:an:backbone}

Table~\ref{tab:multi_backbone_results} indicates that the choice of backbone is the single largest lever for the framework. DINOv2 dominates the supervised alternatives by 5--35 accuracy points on CIFAR-100, ImageNet-R and Joint; ViT-base/16 in particular collapses to 43--46\,\% on ImageNet-R. The empirical pattern correlates with the Gaussianity statistics of Table~\ref{tab:gaussianity_stats}: DINOv2 produces approximately Gaussian, well-conditioned embeddings, whereas supervised ViTs trained on a fixed labelled corpus tend to produce more anisotropic features that violate the modelling assumption. Caltech-256 is the only exception: the three backbones cluster within four accuracy points, with ViT-medium narrowly ahead. This pattern is consistent with the interpretation that Caltech-256 is sufficiently easy on the visual side that the bottleneck moves from the encoder to the downstream classifier; the choice of backbone matters less when the categories are already linearly separable in the feature space.

\section{Sample Efficiency of the Closed-Form Estimators}
\label{sec:an:sample-eff}

Figure~\ref{fig:incremental_class_inclusion} reports the evolution of the Diag-Cov and Full-Cov classifiers as the training samples of a new class become available in steps of $10\,\%$ of the per-class budget. The patterns reveal a clean trade-off between parameter budget and information content. On CIFAR-100, the diagonal estimator reaches its asymptote with as few as $50$ samples (the first slice), reflecting the small number of parameters it must estimate; the full-covariance estimator improves more gradually and overtakes Diag-Cov between $40$ and $60\,\%$ of the available samples. On ImageNet-R the trade-off changes: the per-class budget after the train/test split is approximately $40$ samples, and the Full-Cov estimator saturates early at a higher absolute level without benefiting substantially from additional data. On Caltech-256, where the per-class budget is the smallest of the three but the visual variability is the highest, Full-Cov already dominates from the first $10\,\%$ slice, indicating that even partial knowledge of inter-coordinate correlations is informative; Diag-Cov tracks it at a small but constant gap. These observations refine the recommendation of Section~\ref{sec:an:vs}: the Hybrid estimator is the right default when the training budget is large and the covariance is well-conditioned, whereas Diag-Cov should be preferred in the small-sample regime, and Full-Cov is competitive when partial second-order information is sufficient to separate the classes.

\section{Statistical Characterisation of the Embedding Space and Probabilistic Calibration}
\label{sec:an:embedding}

A class-conditional Gaussian classifier rests on three implicit assumptions about the underlying feature space: that the embedding is approximately isotropic at the global scale, that the marginal distribution of every coordinate is approximately normal within each class, and that the per-class covariance matrix can be reliably estimated from the available sample budget. This section examines each of these assumptions in turn, drawing on the empirical evidence summarised in Tables~\ref{tab:gaussianity_stats} and~\ref{tab:dataset_summary} together with Figures~\ref{fig:pca_eigenvalues}, \ref{fig:mahalanobis_full_cov}, \ref{fig:mahalanobis_diag_1d} and~\ref{fig:kl_divergence}.

\subsection{Benchmark Properties}
\label{sec:an:datasets}

The four benchmarks evaluated in this thesis span a continuum of increasing statistical difficulty for a Gaussian classifier. Their salient properties are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:dataset_summary}.

The three primary datasets differ along two largely orthogonal axes: visual complexity (which determines how heavy-tailed the embedding coordinates are) and sample budget (which determines how reliably the per-class covariance can be estimated). CIFAR-100 occupies the favourable corner of both axes, ImageNet-R adds a deliberate input-distribution shift, and Caltech-256 combines heavy tails with a small per-class budget. The Joint benchmark inherits the worst feature of each of the three constituent datasets and is therefore the most informative regime for separating method behaviour, as discussed later in Section~\ref{sec:an:reranking}.

\paragraph{Split CIFAR-100 (low difficulty).}
The DINOv2 features extracted from CIFAR-100 satisfy the marginal-normality assumption to a remarkable degree: 96.1\,\% of the 76\,800 (class, coordinate) pairs lie below the conventional symmetry cut-off and 98.2\,\% of them fall within $|\text{Kurt}|<1$. The mean skewness is essentially zero ($-0.001$), so the empirical mean is a faithful estimate of the class prototype. Combined with a generous training budget of approximately five hundred samples per class, this benchmark constitutes the regime in which every covariance estimator can be expected to behave well. Performance differences between methods on this dataset (typically below three accuracy points in Table~\ref{tab:combined_benchmarks}) should therefore be interpreted as a measure of the calibration of the model rather than of its representational capacity.

\paragraph{Split ImageNet-R (moderate difficulty).}
ImageNet-R was deliberately constructed to test out-of-distribution robustness by mixing artistic renditions, cartoons and sketches of common ImageNet categories~\cite{imagenetR}. From a modelling perspective this design choice manifests as a marked broadening of the marginal distributions: the standard deviation of the skewness almost doubles relative to CIFAR-100, and the fraction of approximately symmetric coordinates drops to 85.9\,\%. The excess kurtosis is more dispersed as well, with $|\text{Kurt}|_\mu = 0.43$ versus $0.26$ on CIFAR-100. Methods that rely on the volume of the Gaussian---specifically, on the log-determinant---are therefore expected to suffer here, while estimators that emphasise the shape of the class manifold should remain competitive.

\paragraph{Split Caltech-256 (high difficulty).}
Caltech-256 represents the most demanding scenario for a class-conditional Gaussian model. We use the first 250 of its 256 categories so that the protocol can be divided into 10 disjoint tasks of 25 classes each; the resulting subset contains approximately 28\,100 images and yields an average per-class budget of $\overline{n_c}\!\approx\!112$, well below the embedding dimensionality $d=768$. The mean absolute skewness rises to 0.36 and the mean absolute excess kurtosis to 0.66, indicating noticeably heavier tails, while only 74.7\,\% of the coordinates clear the symmetry threshold. Two consequences follow: the empirical sample covariance matrix is severely rank-deficient and demands regularisation, and a single Gaussian per class becomes too rigid for the categories with the highest intra-class variance. Both observations recur explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:an:models}.

\paragraph{Split Joint (long-horizon stress test).}
The Joint stream concatenates the three benchmarks above into a single 30-task sequence whose classes are presented in randomised order. This setup inherits the worst statistical property of each constituent benchmark: rank deficiency from Caltech-256, heavy tails from ImageNet-R, large class count, and rapid distribution drift between consecutive tasks. As shown in Section~\ref{sec:an:reranking}, the Joint benchmark is the most informative regime for distinguishing between re-ranking strategies precisely because it pushes the Gaussian assumption against its operational limits.

\subsection{Spectral Properties of the Foundation Embeddings}
\label{sec:an:spectrum}

The PCA spectrum of Figure~\ref{fig:pca_eigenvalues} provides a dataset-agnostic characterisation of the DINOv2 feature space. Three regularities deserve attention. First, all three benchmarks share a remarkably similar eigenvalue profile despite their disparate visual content: a sharp decay over the first $\sim\!50$ components captures approximately 36--40\,\% of the total variance, followed by a long, slowly-decaying tail across the remaining 700+ components. This indicates that DINOv2 produces a representation whose informative directions are concentrated in a moderate-dimensional subspace but whose residual variability is spread across the full ambient dimensionality. Second, the absence of an abrupt drop in the spectrum implies that the embedding is not confined to a low-dimensional manifold; the discriminative signal is distributed across all directions, which justifies the choice of a high-dimensional ($d=768$) generative model rather than a PCA-reduced surrogate. Third, the effective rank of the per-class empirical covariance is upper-bounded by $n_c - 1$ regardless of dataset, because at most $n_c - 1$ directions can be populated by $n_c$ centred samples. As discussed below, this single algebraic fact is the root cause of the deviations between the empirical and theoretical Mahalanobis distributions reported in Figures~\ref{fig:mahalanobis_full_cov} and~\ref{fig:mahalanobis_diag_1d}.

\subsection{Inter-Class Separability}
\label{sec:an:interclass}

The pairwise KL divergences plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:kl_divergence} extend the diagnostic from the per-class level to the level of class pairs and provide a quantitative measure of how distinguishable the class-conditional Gaussians are. As a reference for interpreting the scale: pairs with KL~$>100$ are well-separated and reliably classified; pairs with $20 \le \mathrm{KL} \le 100$ show moderate overlap with occasional errors; pairs with KL~$<20$ are strongly overlapping and constitute the primary confusion sources. Three observations refine the dataset characterisation of Section~\ref{sec:an:datasets}. First, the absolute scale of the average pairwise KL divergence is markedly dataset-dependent: Caltech-256 reaches ${\sim}993$, CIFAR-100 stabilises at ${\sim}371$ and ImageNet-R at ${\sim}356$. The Caltech-256 figure reflects a regime in which heavy-tailed coordinates inflate the variance and trace terms of the KL even though the underlying Gaussian assumption is more strained---a useful reminder that high pairwise KL is not the same as high model fidelity. Second, the average KL curve plateaus very quickly (after $\sim\!30$ classes have been accumulated) on every benchmark, indicating that the bulk of the class space remains well separated as additional classes are introduced and that the average distinguishability is essentially insensitive to the number of classes. Third, and most consequentially, the \emph{minimum}-KL curve decays sharply on every benchmark: even on CIFAR-100, where the average separability is moderate, the minimum KL falls to $14$ once all 100 classes are considered, placing the hardest pair in the strong-overlap regime. Over $99.7\,\%$ of all class pairs remain above KL~$=100$ on every benchmark, confirming that the classifier's challenge is concentrated in the small tail of near-overlapping pairs. The Bayesian classifier is therefore not faced with a uniformly indistinguishable class space but with a small subset of class pairs whose Gaussians strongly overlap. Crucially, the co-evolution of the minimum-KL curve and the per-task accuracy mirrors the pattern identified in the Gaussian density estimation literature: both the average pairwise KL divergence and the average classification accuracy degrade gradually and smoothly as the number of classes grows, while specific class combinations cause more pronounced accuracy drops---precisely those pairs whose Gaussians lie in the strong-overlap regime. The same phenomenon underlies the gradual increase in forgetting observed in Table~\ref{tab:combined_benchmarks} as the task index grows: the model is not forgetting in the conventional sense but is increasingly confronted with intrinsically confusable pairs that second-order statistics alone cannot resolve. This is precisely the regime where the additive log-domain re-rankers of Section~\ref{sec:an:reranking} most effectively contribute.

\subsection{Empirical Validation of the Gaussian Assumption}
\label{sec:an:gauss}

\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.97\linewidth]{imagenes/results/mahalanobis_diag_and_1d.png}
	\caption{Squared Mahalanobis distance histograms across the three benchmarks. \textbf{Top row (a$_1$--a$_3$):} Diag-Cov histograms vs.\ the theoretical $\chi^2_{d=768}$. \textbf{Bottom row (b$_1$--b$_3$):} single-component histograms vs.\ $\chi^2_1$. The 1-D distributions closely match the reference on every dataset; the multivariate dispersion in the top row reflects unmodelled inter-coordinate correlations.}
	\label{fig:mahalanobis_diag_1d}
\end{figure}

Two complementary diagnostics confirm that the Gaussian assumption is empirically defensible at the per-coordinate level and that the discrepancies observed in the multivariate regime are an artefact of the estimator rather than of the data. The Diag-Cov histograms in the top row of Figure~\ref{fig:mahalanobis_diag_1d} are broad and centred near the theoretical mode $d-2 \approx 766$ on every dataset, with a dispersion that grows monotonically from CIFAR-100 to ImageNet-R to Caltech-256---an ordering that matches the heavy-tail ordering of Table~\ref{tab:gaussianity_stats}. The single-coordinate histograms in the bottom row track the theoretical $\chi^2_{1}$ density very closely across all three benchmarks, providing direct evidence that the marginal Gaussianity assumption is well supported at the level of individual feature components.

The Full-Cov histograms in Figure~\ref{fig:mahalanobis_full_cov} tell a strikingly different story. The empirical mass collapses to a sharp spike near $\overline{n_c}-1$---approximately $498$ on CIFAR-100, $168$ on ImageNet-R and $103$ on Caltech-256---rather than approaching the expected $\chi^2_{d=768}$ density. The location of the spike scales \emph{linearly} with the per-class sample budget, which is the unmistakable fingerprint of estimating a $d\times d$ covariance from $n_c<d$ samples: the eigenvalues that fall outside the spanned subspace are pinned to the regularisation floor $\epsilon$, the inverse blows up along those directions, and the resulting quadratic form becomes degenerate. The crucial implication is that the multivariate discrepancy is a property of the \emph{estimator}, not of the data: the single-coordinate panels of the same figure demonstrate that the underlying coordinates are nearly Gaussian, and the multivariate mismatch reflects the diagonal approximation (which ignores off-diagonal correlations) and rank deficiency (which destabilises the full-rank inverse), not a violation of the per-component normality assumption.

These results lead to a precise characterisation of the two main covariance estimators. The \textbf{Diag-Cov} model avoids rank deficiency entirely---its $d$ parameters can always be estimated stably regardless of the per-class sample budget---and is therefore inherently robust in class-imbalanced and low-data settings. However, it ignores inter-feature correlations by construction and cannot capture the full joint feature distribution, which is why the multivariate Mahalanobis distances depart from the $\chi^2_d$ reference even when the per-coordinate marginals are well-behaved. The \textbf{Full-Cov} model captures those correlations through the complete $d\times d$ precision matrix, but its reliance on the full second-order structure leads to rank-deficient covariance estimates whenever $n_c < d$---precisely the regime encountered on ImageNet-R and Caltech-256. The effective covariance rank then depends on the available samples, making Full-Cov sensitive to class imbalance. Developing a principled strategy that preserves inter-feature correlations while avoiding this rank-deficiency pathology is the key design challenge addressed by the Hybrid estimator introduced in this thesis.

\subsection{Probabilistic Calibration as a Quality of the Volume Term}
\label{sec:an:calibration}

The ECE column of Table~\ref{tab:calibration_metrics} provides an independent view of the volume--shape trade-off discussed in Section~\ref{sec:an:vs}. The diagonal estimator is systematically better calibrated than the full-covariance one across all benchmarks. The gap is already substantial on CIFAR-100 (approximately 8 percentage points: $3.72\,\%$ versus $11.68\,\%$), widens further on ImageNet-R ($13.72\,\%$ versus $37.44\,\%$, a difference of $23.7$ points) and on Caltech-256 ($7.05\,\%$ versus $26.03\,\%$, a difference of $19.0$ points), and remains significant on the Joint stream ($11.46\,\%$ versus $29.05\,\%$, a gap of $17.6$ percentage points). The explanation is, once again, that the full-rank log-determinant in $d=768$ produces extreme volume scores; once they are normalised through the softmax, the posterior collapses onto a handful of classes regardless of the actual quality of the prediction. Temperature scaling alone is insufficient to repair this collapse in the long-horizon regime, which is the reason why the methodology recommends Hybrid-Cov as the calibration-aware default rather than Full-Cov.

The complementary metrics reported in Table~\ref{tab:calibration_metrics} make the diagnostic richer. The Diag-Cov branch behaves in a numerically reasonable regime on every benchmark: Total Confidence remains between $0.84$ and $0.97$, Confidence on Errors stays well below one (between $0.47$ and $0.81$), the Log-Gap between the top-1 and top-2 log-likelihoods is bounded by approximately $5$ and $155$, and the predictive entropy is non-trivial ($0.07$--$0.61$). The Full-Cov branch, by contrast, saturates to a degenerate confidence regime on every benchmark: Total Confidence equals $1.0000$, Confidence on Errors collapses to approximately one (so the model is just as confident when it is wrong as when it is right), entropy drops below $10^{-3}$, and the Log-Gap explodes to values between $5\times 10^{3}$ on ImageNet-R and $2.7\times 10^{6}$ on the Joint stream, reaching $8.9\times 10^{7}$ even on CIFAR-100. The numerical signature of this collapse is the same one identified in Section~\ref{sec:an:gauss}: the rank-deficient determinant produces astronomically peaked posteriors that are essentially deterministic regardless of input.

A particularly informative consequence of this analysis is that the Full-Cov branch attains \emph{higher} final-task accuracy than Diag-Cov on CIFAR-100 ($88.3\,\%$ versus $86.4\,\%$) while being miscalibrated by eight additional ECE points. This is the cleanest empirical illustration in the thesis of the principle that classification accuracy and probabilistic calibration are largely orthogonal in the rank-deficient regime: the volume-driven peaking that destroys calibration does not necessarily destroy accuracy, provided the per-class shapes are still discriminative. On the harder benchmarks the rank-deficient determinant becomes actively harmful and Full-Cov loses accuracy on top of being miscalibrated, which is why Hybrid-Cov is the recommended default.

\section{Computational Footprint and Limitations}
\label{sec:an:other}

\subsection{Computational Footprint and Practical Scalability}
\label{sec:an:compute}

A side observation that is easy to overlook in the tables of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} is that every method proposed in this thesis is closed-form. The training cost is dominated by the single forward pass through the frozen DINOv2 backbone and by the closed-form estimation of the per-class means and covariances. By contrast, the prompt-based competitors require gradient updates of the prompt pool at every incremental task. On CIFAR-100, adding a new task to our framework requires approximately two minutes on a single consumer-grade GPU, almost entirely spent on feature extraction; prompt-based methods reported in the literature typically require 10--30 minutes per task on comparable hardware. The proposed framework therefore offers a 5--15$\times$ speedup at training time, without any rehearsal storage requirement.

\subsection{Limitations and Open Questions}
\label{sec:an:limits}

The present evaluation has four limitations that deserve explicit acknowledgement. First, the Joint benchmark assumes that task boundaries are known at training time; relaxing this assumption (task-free continual learning) is a natural follow-up that would test the robustness of the additive task-level priors of Section~\ref{sec:an:soft}. Second, the re-ranking strategies do not currently exploit the per-task Gaussianity statistics reported in Table~\ref{tab:gaussianity_stats}; an adaptive fusion in which the weight $\alpha$ depends on the empirical kurtosis of the active classes is a promising extension. Third, the evaluation is restricted to visual classification: although the framework is in principle agnostic to the modality, an empirical assessment on text or speech embeddings would provide a valuable external test of the underlying Gaussian assumption. Fourth, a more speculative direction would be to extend the framework to open-world settings by treating the predictive entropy and Log-Gap of Table~\ref{tab:calibration_metrics} as out-of-distribution scores; the results of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} suggest that the Diag-Cov branch is the only one in which these quantities are meaningful.

\section{Synthesis and Practical Recommendations}
\label{sec:an:summary}

The experimental evidence collected in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} supports four high-level conclusions that, taken together, justify the design choices of the proposed framework.

\paragraph{Finding 1: Gaussian class-conditional modelling is a viable design choice on modern self-supervised embeddings.}
The DINOv2 features satisfy the marginal-normality assumption on 96\,\% of the coordinates in CIFAR-100, 86\,\% in ImageNet-R and 75\,\% in Caltech-256, with the residual non-Gaussianity concentrated in the heavy-tailed Caltech-256 case. The single-coordinate Mahalanobis histograms (Figure~\ref{fig:mahalanobis_diag_1d}b) confirm that the per-component distributions track the theoretical $\chi^2_{1}$ density. The pairwise KL divergences of Figure~\ref{fig:kl_divergence} additionally show that the average distinguishability between class Gaussians is large and stable across all class counts, while the minimum KL diagnoses the small set of intrinsically confusable pairs that account for the residual forgetting. A classifier built directly on top of these statistics achieves performance competitive with prompt-based state of the art while remaining closed-form, deterministic and gradient-free.

\paragraph{Finding 2: The Hybrid covariance is the most useful single estimator developed in this thesis.}
By combining the discriminative power of the full-rank Mahalanobis distance with the calibration properties of the diagonal log-determinant, the Hybrid estimator outperforms every alternative on the heavy-tailed and long-horizon benchmarks (Caltech-256 and Joint), and matches the best linear shrinkage estimators on CIFAR-100. The linear shrinkage estimators (OAS, Ledoit--Wolf) repair the covariance for estimation purposes but flatten the eigenvalue spectrum that the classifier exploits, and they are dominated by Hybrid in every regime that is not trivially in-distribution. In doing so, the Hybrid estimator directly addresses the open problem of developing a principled covariance estimation strategy that preserves inter-feature correlations while remaining stable under rank deficiency---a challenge identified as an important direction for future work in the Gaussian density estimation literature for class-incremental learning~\cite{restuccia2024gaussian}.

\paragraph{Finding 3: Additive log-domain fusion is the only re-ranking pattern that improves the baseline reliably.}
The strategies SoftTaskFus, TaskMeanShr, OptFusion-MM, Top5-CosRR and TempScale consistently improve, or at worst match, the unfused baseline, with the largest absolute gain ($3.3$ accuracy points on Split Joint with Full-Cov) achieved by OptFusion-MM. Hard-gating strategies (HardGate, Top5-TaskRR, TopK-TaskFilt), by contrast, destroy accuracy whenever the task-level Gaussian is mis-calibrated---a condition that arises precisely on the benchmarks where re-ranking would otherwise be most needed.

\paragraph{Finding 4: The framework's accuracy ceiling is set by the upstream representation, not by the downstream classifier.}
The empirical gap between DINOv2 and supervised ViT variants in Table~\ref{tab:multi_backbone_results} is larger than any gap induced by the choice of covariance estimator or re-ranker. The pragmatic implication is that future improvements should target the upstream representation rather than the downstream classifier: a more discriminative, better-conditioned encoder will lift the entire framework without requiring methodological changes.
