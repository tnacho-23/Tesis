\section{Proposed Architecture}

As shown in \ref{fig:architecture}, our CIL framework is built upon a simple yet structurally motivated decomposition: (i) a frozen feature extractor based on a visual foundation model, and (ii) a probabilistic classification layer that models each object category through an independent Gaussian distribution in the embedding space. Given an input image, the feature extractor maps it to a fixed high-dimensional representation. For each class, we maintain a class-conditional multivariate Gaussian characterized by its mean vector and covariance matrix, estimated incrementally from observed samples. Classification is performed according to the maximum likelihood principle, assigning the input to the class whose Gaussian model yields the highest likelihood under the stationary embedding representation. This modular separation between representation learning and statistical modeling enables incremental updates without backpropagation or parameter adaptation: adding a new class requires only estimating its distribution in the fixed feature space, while previously learned classes remain unaffected at the representation level.

\subsection{Likelihood-Based Classification}
\label{sec:method:classification}

Given an input image $x \in \mathcal{X}$, the frozen foundation model $\mathcal{F}$ produces a fixed-dimensional embedding
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z} = \mathcal{F}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d, \quad d = 768,
    \label{eq:feature_extractor}
\end{equation}
and all learning is performed directly in the stationary feature space $\mathbb{R}^d$. We adopt a generative modelling framework and assume class-conditional Gaussian feature distributions:
\begin{equation}
    p(\mathbf{z} \mid y = c) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_c,\, \Sigma_c),
    \label{eq:class_gaussian}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $\Sigma_c \succ 0$ are the class-specific mean and covariance matrix, estimated incrementally from observed samples. Classification is performed via Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) inference:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y} = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{Y}} \left[ \log p(\mathbf{z} \mid y=c) + \log \pi_c \right],
    \label{eq:map}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_c = p(y=c)$ is the class prior. Under a uniform prior and the Gaussian density model, the decision rule is equivalent to the quadratic discriminant:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y} = \arg\min_{c \in \mathcal{Y}} \left[ (\mathbf{z} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^T \Sigma_c^{-1} (\mathbf{z} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c) + \log|\Sigma_c| \right].
    \label{eq:qda}
\end{equation}
This quadratic decision function is the core of the proposed framework. The Mahalanobis term $(\mathbf{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^T\Sigma_c^{-1}(\mathbf{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_c)$ encodes the \emph{shape} of the class distribution while $\log|\Sigma_c|$ encodes its \emph{volume}. The covariance parameterisations introduced in Section~\ref{sec:covariance_estimation} are specialisations of Equation~\eqref{eq:qda} that differ in the structure imposed on $\Sigma_c$; the interplay between the shape and volume components is the central design axis of the covariance estimation study and is examined in detail in the analysis of results.

Adding a new class to the system requires only estimating the sufficient statistics $(\boldsymbol{\mu}_c, \Sigma_c)$ in the fixed feature space; previously learned class statistics are never modified. This formulation therefore enables genuinely incremental updates without backpropagation, replay buffers, or exemplar storage.

\subsection{Datasets and Experimental Protocol}
We follow the standard Continual Learning (CL) protocol where the model learns a sequence of disjoint tasks and calculates the average accuracy and the forgetting. The specific configurations for the datasets are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Split CIFAR-100:} This dataset is a widely utilized benchmark in the continual learning literature. It partitions the original CIFAR-100 \cite{cifar100} dataset into 10 disjoint tasks, with each task containing 10 distinct classes.
	
	\item \textbf{Split ImageNet-R:} This benchmark is constructed from ImageNet-R \cite{imagenetR}, a dataset containing renditions of ImageNet classes such as art, cartoons, and sketches. We randomly divide the 200 classes into 10 sequential tasks, with 20 classes per task, introducing a significant domain shift challenge.
	
	\item \textbf{Split Caltech-256:} Designed to evaluate the model on higher-resolution images with greater object variability, this benchmark utilises the Caltech-256 \cite{caltech256} dataset. The first 250 of the original 256 categories are used and sequentially divided into 10 disjoint tasks of 25 classes each; the remaining six categories are discarded so that the benchmark has a fixed, balanced number of classes per task. The resulting protocol contains approximately 28\,100 images with an average of $\overline{n_c}\!\approx\!112$ training samples per class, which makes the per-class budget the smallest of the three constituent datasets and stresses the high-dimensional covariance estimators introduced in Section~3.2.
	
	\item \textbf{Split Joint Dataset:} This benchmark serves as a comprehensive stress test by aggregating the 30 tasks from the three aforementioned datasets into a single, heterogeneous stream. The tasks are presented to the system in a randomized order, forcing the model to navigate extreme distribution shifts between natural, artistic, and high-resolution images. This long-horizon sequence is specifically designed to evaluate the model's resilience against catastrophic forgetting over an extended period of diverse learning objectives.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model Metrics}

As mentioned, to quantify performance, SOTA works \cite{wang2022learning} utilize two standard metrics derived from the test accuracy scores: the average accuracy and the average forgetting.

On the one hand, \textbf{Average Accuracy ($A_t$)}, presented on equation \eqref{eq_AA}, measures overall performance after task $t$. It is the mean test accuracy across all tasks learned up to that point.

\begin{equation}
	A_t = \frac{1}{t} \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} S_{t,\tau}
	\label{eq_AA}
\end{equation}

On the other hand, \textbf{Forgetting ($F_{t}$)}, presented on equation \eqref{eq_AF}, quantifies how much the model has forgotten earlier tasks. It computes, for each past task, how much accuracy has dropped compared to its best historical score, and averages those drops.

\begin{equation}
	F_t = \frac{1}{t-1} \sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1} \left( \max_{k \le t-1} S_{k,\tau} - S_{t,\tau} \right)
	\label{eq_AF}
\end{equation}



Regarding forgetting, the proposed framework does not exhibit forgetting in the conventional sense, as previously learned class statistics are never modified when new classes are introduced. The measured forgetting values therefore primarily reflect the increased classification difficulty as the number of competing classes grows, rather than degradation of previously learned representations.

\section{Covariance Estimation}
\label{sec:covariance_estimation}

As mentioned, due to the high the dimensionality of the extracted feature, the empirical sample covariance matrix (SCM) is a poor estimator: its eigenvalues are biased, and the matrix is singular if $n < d$. Our benchmarks are tested using different convariance estimation methods:


\subsection{Diagonal Covariances}
In this configuration, we assume that the features within each class $c$ are independent, leading to a diagonal covariance matrix $\Sigma_c = \text{diag}(\sigma_{c,1}^2, \sigma_{c,2}^2, \dots, \sigma_{c,d}^2)$. This simplification significantly reduces the number of parameters to estimate from $O(d^2)$ to $O(d)$, which is particularly advantageous when the dimensionality $d = 768$ is large relative to the number of samples per class.For each class $c \in \{1, \dots, C\}$, the mean vector $\mu_c$ and the diagonal elements of $\Sigma_c$ are computed as:

\begin{equation}
	\mu_c = \frac{1}{n_c} \sum_{i=1}^{n_c} x_i, \quad \sigma_{c,j}^2 = \frac{1}{n_c} \sum_{i=1}^{n_c} (x_{i,j} - \mu_{c,j})^2 + \epsilon
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ is a small constant added to ensure numerical stability and strictly positive variances. The log-likelihood of a test sample $\mathbf{x}$ belonging to class $c$ is determined by the discriminant function:

\begin{equation}
	g_c(\mathbf{x}) = \ln P(\mathbf{x}|c) + \ln P(c)
\end{equation}

Under the assumption of a uniform prior $P(c)$ and Gaussian distribution, this simplifies to:

\begin{equation}
	g_c(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{d} \left( \ln(2\pi\sigma_{c,j}^2) + \frac{(x_j - \mu_{c,j})^2}{\sigma_{c,j}^2} \right)
\end{equation}

In the implementation, this is computed using a pre-calculated constant $\ln A_c = -\frac{d}{2}\ln(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2}\sum \ln(\sigma_{c,j}^2)$ and the squared Mahalanobis distance weighted by the inverse of the diagonal variances.

\paragraph{Score component analysis.}
To characterise how each term contributes to classification, the two components of the score are evaluated separately on the held-out validation partition. Gaussian parameters are estimated exclusively from the training partition and remain fixed. At evaluation time, every validation sample is scored against \emph{all} $C$ classes simultaneously, not only its ground-truth class. This produces a $C$-dimensional vector of volume terms $\{\ln A_c\}$ (one value per class, independent of any test sample) and a full $N_{\mathrm{val}} \times C$ matrix for both $d_{\mathrm{mah}}(\mathbf{x},c)$ and $\mathrm{score}(\mathbf{x},c)$. Inspecting the marginal distributions of these matrices reveals whether the classifier's decisions are dominated by the volume penalty (covariance geometry) or by the shape term (distance to the class mean), and whether the two components interact as expected under the Gaussian model.

For the \textbf{diagonal-covariance} estimator the full $N_{\mathrm{val}} \times C$ matrix is computed in closed form via vectorised operations, since the precision matrix is diagonal. For the \textbf{full-covariance} estimator, the analysis is carried out on a random subsample of the validation partition to keep the computation tractable.


\subsection{Full Covariances}

To account for the dependencies between different dimensions of the feature vector, we estimate a full covariance matrix $\Sigma_c \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ for each class $c$. While this provides a richer representation of the class distribution, the estimation of $d^2$ parameters (where $d=768$) is prone to ill-conditioning when the number of samples $n_c$ is limited.The mean vector $\mu_c$ and the empirical covariance $\Sigma_c$ are calculated as:

\begin{equation}
	\mu_c = \frac{1}{n_c} \sum_{i=1}^{n_c} x_i, \quad \Sigma_c = \frac{1}{n_c - 1} \sum_{i=1}^{n_c} (x_i - \mu_c)(x_i - \mu_c)^T
\end{equation}

To ensure the matrix is invertible and to improve numerical stability, a regularizatio is applied by adding a small identity term to the diagonal: $\hat{\Sigma}_c = \Sigma_c + \epsilon I$, with $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$.The implementation performs the inversion and determinant calculation using the eigendecomposition of the symmetric covariance matrix, $\hat{\Sigma}_c = P \Lambda P^T$, where $\Lambda$ contains the eigenvalues $\lambda_j$. To further ensure stability, eigenvalues are clipped to a minimum value of $10^{-6}$. The inverse and the log-determinant are then computed as:
\begin{equation}
	\hat{\Sigma}_c^{-1} = P \Lambda^{-1} P^T, \quad \ln |\hat{\Sigma}_c| = \sum_{j=1}^{d} \ln(\lambda_j)
\end{equation}

The discriminant function for classification is the full quadratic form:
\begin{equation}
	g_c(\mathbf{x}) = \ln A_c - \frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^T \hat{\Sigma}_c^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)
\end{equation}

where $\ln A_c = -\frac{1}{2} (d \ln(2\pi) + \ln |\hat{\Sigma}_c|)$.

\subsection{Covariance Conditioning Analysis}
\label{sec:method:conditioning}

The sample covariance matrix $\hat{\Sigma}_c \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ estimated from $n_c$ training samples is rank-deficient whenever $n_c < d$.
In our setting $d = 768$ while $n_c$ ranges from approximately $40$ to $400$ depending on the benchmark, so the matrix always has at most $n_c - 1$ non-zero eigenvalues out of $d = 768$, leaving $d - (n_c - 1)$ eigenvalues that are structurally zero before any regularisation is applied.
To quantify this ill-conditioning we analyse the eigenvalue spectrum of the regularised matrix $\hat{\Sigma}_c^{\text{reg}} = \hat{\Sigma}_c + \varepsilon I$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$, and compute the following metrics per class.

\paragraph{Condition number.}
The condition number is defined as the ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue:
\begin{equation}
	\kappa(\hat{\Sigma}_c^{\text{reg}}) = \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}},
	\label{eq:condition_number}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\max}$ and $\lambda_{\min}$ are the extremal eigenvalues of $\hat{\Sigma}_c^{\text{reg}}$.
A large $\kappa$ indicates that the matrix is nearly singular in the sense that directions associated with small eigenvalues contribute negligible variance and inflate the Mahalanobis distance.
We report $\log_{10}(\kappa)$ to compress the dynamic range.

\paragraph{Effective dimensions.}
A dimension $j$ is classified as \emph{effective} if $\lambda_j \geq \varepsilon_{\mathrm{thr}} = 10^{-4}$, and as \emph{degenerate} otherwise.
The number of degenerate dimensions per class measures directly how many of the $d = 768$ directions are driven to the regularisation floor and thus carry no meaningful covariance information.

\paragraph{Effective rank.}
The entropy-based effective rank~\cite{roy2007effective} captures the spread of the eigenvalue distribution:
\begin{equation}
	r_{\mathrm{eff}}(\hat{\Sigma}_c^{\text{reg}})
	= \exp\!\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{d} p_j \ln p_j\right),
	\qquad p_j = \frac{\lambda_j}{\sum_{k}\lambda_k}.
	\label{eq:effective_rank}
\end{equation}
This equals $d$ for a perfectly isotropic matrix ($\lambda_j = \mathrm{const}$) and equals $1$ when a single eigenvalue dominates.
In practice it provides a soft count of the directions that carry the bulk of the variance.

\subsection{Cross-Class Frobenius Norm Analysis}
\label{sec:method:frobenius}

To characterise the geometric diversity of the class-conditional covariance matrices after regularisation, we compute all pairwise Frobenius distances:
\begin{equation}
	D_{ij}^F = \|\hat{\Sigma}_i^{\text{reg}} - \hat{\Sigma}_j^{\text{reg}}\|_F
	= \sqrt{\sum_{k,l}\!\left(\hat{\sigma}_{i,kl} - \hat{\sigma}_{j,kl}\right)^2},
	\label{eq:frobenius_dist}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\Sigma}_c^{\text{reg}} = \hat{\Sigma}_c + \gamma I$, $\gamma = 10^{-6}$.
The resulting $C \times C$ symmetric matrix $\mathbf{D}^F$ with zero diagonal captures how similar or dissimilar any two class covariances are in the ambient Frobenius geometry.

A small $D_{ij}^F$ indicates that classes $i$ and $j$ share nearly identical second-order statistics, implying that the Mahalanobis-based classifier must rely primarily on the mean displacement $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j$ rather than covariance contrast.
Conversely, a large $D_{ij}^F$ indicates that the covariance geometry itself provides additional discriminative information beyond the first-order term.

We summarise the resulting distribution via its mean, standard deviation, and percentile bands, and visualise the full $C \times C$ heatmap for each benchmark.
Results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:covariance_structure_results}.

\subsection{Hybrid Covariance}
The hybrid covariance estimator decouples the estimation of the Mahalanobis distance from the normalisation constant.For each class $c$, the full covariance matrix $\hat{\Sigma}_c$ is estimated and regularized as described in the previous section:

\begin{equation}
	\hat{\Sigma}_c = \Sigma_c + \epsilon I
\end{equation}
The quadratic form of the log-likelihood (the Mahalanobis distance) uses the full inverse $\hat{\Sigma}_c^{-1}$, calculated via eigendecomposition to capture the feature interactions. However, the log-determinant—which represents the "volume" of the distribution—is computed using only the diagonal elements of the covariance matrix (the variances):
\begin{equation}
	\ln |\Sigma_c|_{\text{diag}} = \sum_{j=1}^{d} \ln(\sigma_{c,j}^2)
\end{equation}

where $\sigma_{c,j}^2$ are the diagonal entries of $\hat{\Sigma}_c$. The resulting discriminant function is defined as:
\begin{equation}
	g_c(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{1}{2} \left( d \ln(2\pi) + \sum_{j=1}^{d} \ln(\sigma_{c,j}^2) + (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^T \hat{\Sigma}_c^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c) \right)
\end{equation}

This formulation avoids the numerical issues that arise when very small eigenvalues inflate the log-determinant, at the cost of using an approximation for the volume term.


\subsection{OAS Covariance}

Oracle Approximating Shrinkage (OAS) is a shrinkage technique that computes an structurally optimized covariance matrix by finding an optimal compromise between the sample covariance matrix and a structured target (the identity matrix scaled by the average trace).The OAS estimator provides a more accurate estimation of the covariance $\Sigma_c$ by minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) under the assumption of Gaussianity. The estimated covariance is defined as:

\begin{equation}
	\hat{\Sigma}_{\text{OAS}, c} = (1 - \rho_c) \hat{\Sigma}_{\text{SCM}, c} + \rho_c \hat{F}_c
\end{equation}
where $\rho_c$ is the shrinkage coefficient calculated specifically for class $c$, $\hat{\Sigma}_{\text{SCM}, c}$ is the sample covariance matrix, and $\hat{F}_c = \frac{\text{tr}(\hat{\Sigma}_{\text{SCM}, c})}{d} I$ is the shrinkage target.In our implementation, we compute the precision matrix $\Psi_c = \hat{\Sigma}_{\text{OAS}, c}^{-1}$ directly via the OAS algorithm. This precision matrix is then used to calculate the Mahalanobis distance in the log-likelihood discriminant function:

\begin{equation}
	g_c(\mathbf{x}) = \ln A_c - \frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^T \Psi_c (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)
\end{equation}

where $\ln A_c$ is the normalization constant derived from the log-determinant of the OAS-estimated covariance matrix, evaluated numerically via the stable log-determinant of the eigendecomposition. This approach ensures that the model captures class-specific correlations while remaining robust to the ``curse of dimensionality.''

\subsection{Ledoit-Wolff}

The Ledoit-Wolf Diagonal Anchor estimator addresses the instability of the sample covariance matrix $S$ by shrinking it toward a target matrix $T$.

The selection of a target matrix $T$ represents a prior assumption regarding the feature distribution. Both methods stabilize the estimator by pulling the noisy off-diagonal elements of the sample covariance matrix $S$ toward zero, but they differ in how they treat the diagonal elements (the variances).

\subsubsection{Diagonal Target}
The diagonal anchor is defined as $T_{\text{diag}} = \text{diag}(s_{11}, s_{22}, \dots, s_{dd})$. This approach assumes that the features are independent but allows for heteroscedasticity, meaning each dimension can have a different variance. Shrinkage toward this target preserves the scale of each individual feature as observed in the sample data while suppressing only the correlations. This is particularly useful when certain dimensions of the DINOv2 embeddings are naturally more informative or volatile than others.

\subsubsection{Identity Target}The identity anchor is more restrictive, defined as $T_{\text{ident}} = \mu I$, where $\mu = \frac{1}{d} \text{tr}(S)$ is the average variance across all dimensions. This target assumes that the features are not only independent but also share a common variance (homoscedasticity). The resulting distribution is spherical. Because the embeddings used in this work are L2-normalized, the variances across dimensions tend to be similar, making the identity anchor a highly robust regularizer that prevents any single dimension from disproportionately influencing the distance metric.

\subsubsection{Structural Summary}The fundamental difference between these two estimators lies in the flexibility of the variance components:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Diagonal Anchor:} $\hat{\Sigma}_{ij} = (1-\gamma)s_{ij}$ for $i \neq j$, and $\hat{\Sigma}_{ii} = s_{ii}$. It yields an axis-aligned ellipsoidal distribution.
	\item \textbf{Identity Anchor:} $\hat{\Sigma}_{ij} = (1-\gamma)s_{ij}$ for $i \neq j$, and $\hat{\Sigma}_{ii} = (1-\gamma)s_{ii} + \gamma \mu$. It yields a distribution that is pulled toward a perfectly spherical geometry.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Shared Covariance}The Shared Covariance framework assumes that the internal noise or feature variability is homoscedastic across all classes. In this paradigm, all classes share a single covariance matrix $\Sigma$, which captures the global structure of the feature space. This significantly reduces the total number of parameters to be estimated compared to class-specific models. Depending on the constraints placed on $\Sigma$, we evaluate three distinct variations:

\subsubsection{Shared Diagonal Covariance}

By enforcing a global diagonal covariance matrix $\Sigma = \text{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2, \dots, \sigma_d^2)$, we simplify the model to assume that feature dimensions are independent. The shared variance for each feature $j$ is estimated by pooling the squared residuals from all $C$ classes:

\begin{equation}
	\sigma_j^2 = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{c=1}^{C} \sum_{i=1}^{n_c} (x_{i,j} - \mu_{c,j})^2 + \epsilon
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the total number of training samples and $\mu_{c,j}$ is the mean of class $c$ for feature $j$. The classification of a test sample $\mathbf{x}$ reduces to finding the class $c$ that minimizes the weighted Euclidean distance:

\begin{equation}
	c^* = \arg\min_{c} \sum_{j=1}^{d} \frac{(x_j - \mu_{c,j})^2}{\sigma_j^2}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Shared Full Covariance}In the shared full covariance model (equivalent to the generative assumption of Linear Discriminant Analysis), we estimate a dense matrix $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that captures the correlations between all feature dimensions. To ensure numerical stability in high-dimensional spaces, we utilize the Oracle Approximating Shrinkage (OAS) estimator to compute the pooled covariance. The classification rule utilizes the shared precision matrix $\Psi = \Sigma^{-1}$ to minimize the full squared Mahalanobis distance:

\begin{equation}
	c^* = \arg\min_{c} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^T \Psi (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Shared Hybrid Covariance}

The shared hybrid approach serves as a regularized middle ground. It utilizes the full shared precision matrix $\Psi$ for the quadratic distance calculation to capture feature interactions, but simplifies the normalization constant (the log-determinant) by considering only the diagonal elements of the shared matrix. This prevents the classification scores from being dominated by extreme eigenvalues while still benefiting from the descriptive power of the full correlation structure.

\subsection{Mixture of Gaussians}

In order to capture potential multi-modality within the feature space of a single class, we employ Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM). We implemented the following models using the previously defined diagonal, full and hybrid covariances approaches.

\subsubsection{Mixture of 2 Gaussians}

Forcing each class to be defined as a GMM with $K=2$ components, each class $c$ can be modeled by a probability density function:

\begin{equation}
	p(\mathbf{x}|c) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_{c,k} \mathcal{N}(x | \mu_{c,k}, \Sigma_{c,k})
\end{equation}

where $\pi_{c,k}$ represents the mixing weight of the $k$-th component for class $c$, subject to $\sum \pi_{c,k} = 1$. In this specific variant, the covariance matrices $\Sigma_{c,k}$ are restricted to be diagonal to maintain computational efficiency and prevent over-fitting in the high-dimensional DINOv2 space.The parameters $\{\pi_{c,k}, \mu_{c,k}, \Sigma_{c,k}\}$ are estimated using the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm. To ensure numerical stability, a small constant $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$ is added to the diagonal of each covariance matrix. The log-likelihood for a test sample $\mathbf{x}$ is calculated using the \textit{LogSumExp} operator to avoid underflow:

\begin{equation}
	\ln p(\mathbf{x}|c) = \text{lnaddexp} \left( \ln(\pi_{c,1} \mathcal{N}(x | \mu_{c,1}, \Sigma_{c,1})), \ln(\pi_{c,2} \mathcal{N}(x | \mu_{c,2}, \Sigma_{c,2})) \right)
\end{equation}

In the implementation, this is expanded as:

\begin{equation}
	\ln p(\mathbf{x}|c) = \ln \left( \exp( \ln \pi_{c,1} + \ln A_{c,1} - \frac{1}{2} d_{M,1} ) + \exp( \ln \pi_{c,2} + \ln A_{c,2} - \frac{1}{2} d_{M,2} ) \right)
\end{equation}

where $d_{M,k}$ is the diagonal Mahalanobis distance to the $k$-th component and $\ln A_{c,k}$ is the normalization constant. This approach allows the model to better represent classes that may contain significantly different visual sub-types or clusters within the latent embedding space.


\subsubsection{Mixture of up-to-4 Gaussians}

To adapt to the varying complexity of different classes within the different datasets, we implement a flexible Gaussian Mixture Model where the number of components $K_c$ for each class $c$ is not fixed. Instead, the model selects the most appropriate number of components $K_c \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ by minimizing the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).The BIC is defined as:

\begin{equation}
	\text{BIC} = \ln(n)k - 2\ln(\hat{L})
\end{equation}

where $n$ is the number of samples in the class, $k$ is the number of free parameters to be estimated, and $\hat{L}$ is the maximized value of the likelihood function. By using diagonal covariance matrices, the number of parameters per component is kept low ($2d + 1$), allowing the selection process to favor multi-modal distributions only when the increase in likelihood justifies the additional parameters. For each component $k$ in class $c$, the diagonal covariance $\Sigma_{c,k} = \text{diag}(\sigma_{c,k,1}^2, \dots, \sigma_{c,k,d}^2)$ is regularized with $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$. The classification of a test sample $\mathbf{x}$ involves calculating the weighted log-likelihood across all active classes:

\begin{equation}
	\ln p(\mathbf{x}|c) = \text{LSE}_{k=1}^{K_c} \left( \ln \pi_{c,k} - \frac{1}{2} \left( \sum_{j=1}^{d} \ln(2\pi\sigma_{c,k,j}^2) + \sum_{j=1}^{d} \frac{(x_j - \mu_{c,k,j})^2}{\sigma_{c,k,j}^2} \right) \right)
\end{equation}

where $\text{LSE}$ denotes the LogSumExp operator. This methodology allows for a more granular representation of class distributions—using a single Gaussian for simple, compact classes and up to four Gaussians for classes with high intra-class variance or distinct visual sub-clusters—while preserving the numerical efficiency of diagonal estimators.


\section{Final-State Confusion Matrix Evaluation}
\label{sec:method:confusion_matrix}

The confusion matrix at the final incremental state $T_{\max}$ provides a diagnostic complementary to the scalar accuracy and calibration metrics reported in Sections~\ref{sec:covariance_estimation} and~\ref{sec:method:calibration_gaussianity}. While average accuracy collapses all per-class errors into a single scalar, the confusion matrix reveals the full joint distribution of true and predicted labels, exposing structured misclassification patterns---such as visual or semantic similarity between classes---that are otherwise invisible.


\subsection{Confusion Matrix Definition}

Let $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{M}$ be the held-out test partition, where $y_i \in \mathcal{C}_{T_{\max}}$ is the ground-truth label and $\hat{y}_i = \arg\max_{c} \log p(\mathbf{x}_i \mid c)$ is the label predicted by maximum-likelihood classification. The confusion matrix $\mathbf{M} \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N \times N}$ is defined entry-wise as
\begin{equation}
    M_{c,\hat{c}} = \bigl|\{i : y_i = c \;\wedge\; \hat{y}_i = \hat{c}\}\bigr|, \qquad c, \hat{c} \in \{0, \ldots, N-1\}.
    \label{eq:confusion_matrix}
\end{equation}
Rows index true classes and columns index predicted classes; a perfect classifier yields a diagonal matrix. For visualisation the row-normalised version
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{M}_{c,\hat{c}} = \frac{M_{c,\hat{c}}}{\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{c}'} M_{c,\hat{c}'}}
    \label{eq:confusion_matrix_norm}
\end{equation}
is displayed, so that each row sums to one and represents the empirical misclassification distribution for a single true class.

\subsection{Mahalanobis-Only Inference}
\label{sec:method:mahal_only}

The standard Gaussian classifier assigns each class $c$ the log-posterior score
\begin{equation}
    s_c(\mathbf{x}) = \log A_c - \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^\top \Sigma_c^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c),
    \label{eq:gaussian_score}
\end{equation}
where the log-normalisation constant $\log A_c = -\tfrac{d}{2}\log(2\pi) - \tfrac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma_c|$ depends on the determinant of $\Sigma_c$. For the diagonal model, $\log A_c = -\tfrac{d}{2}\log(2\pi) - \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\log\sigma_{c,j}^2$; for the full model, $\log A_c = -\tfrac{d}{2}\log(2\pi) - \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\log\lambda_{c,k}$, where $\{\lambda_{c,k}\}$ are the eigenvalues of $\Sigma_c$.

The \emph{Mahalanobis-only} variant removes the normalisation term entirely, reducing the decision rule to the squared Mahalanobis distance alone:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{s}_c(\mathbf{x}) = -\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^\top \Sigma_c^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c).
    \label{eq:mahal_score}
\end{equation}
The predicted class is still $\hat{y} = \arg\max_c \tilde{s}_c(\mathbf{x})$. Geometrically, each class boundary is now determined solely by the inverse covariance shape (the ellipsoidal metric) rather than by the combination of shape and volume encoded in $\log A_c$.

This ablation isolates the contribution of the log-determinant term. When all classes share the same covariance (e.g.\ a common LDA-style matrix), $\log A_c$ is identical for all $c$ and cancels in the $\arg\max$; removing it has no effect. Differences emerge when per-class covariances have varying determinants: a class whose feature distribution occupies a large volume has a small $\log A_c$ (large $|\Sigma_c|$), which acts as a penalty in the full Gaussian rule. Removing $\log A_c$ therefore biases decisions toward high-volume classes and can degrade accuracy when class volumes differ meaningfully---as is the case for the diagonal model, where per-dimension variances are learned independently. Conversely, for the full-covariance model on balanced datasets, the eigenvalues tend to be similar across classes (all classes see the same number of training samples and similar covariance structure), so $\log A_c$ is approximately constant and the Mahalanobis-only and full-Gaussian rules are nearly equivalent.

The ablation is implemented by substituting $\log A_c \leftarrow 0$ for all $c$ in the scoring function (Eq.~\ref{eq:gaussian_score}) while keeping the estimated parameters $\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_c, \Sigma_c^{-1}\}$ unchanged.


\section{Feature-Space Characterization and Gaussianity Analysis}
\label{sec:method:gaussianity_main}

\subsection{Probabilistic Classification in High Dimension}
\label{sec:method:highdimdim}

In high-dimensional feature spaces such as those produced by foundational vision models, probabilistic classification faces a systematic calibration challenge. While the Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) decision rule correctly identifies the most likely class, the resulting posterior probabilities $p(y=c \mid \mathbf{x})$ often exhibit extreme overconfidence: the model assigns near-unit probability to its top prediction even when that prediction is wrong, effectively masking the true uncertainty of the classification.

\paragraph{Uncertainty Collapse in High Dimensions}
Under diagonal covariance Gaussian models, the log-likelihood for class $c$ is:
\begin{equation}
	\log p(\mathbf{x} \mid y=c) = \ln \mathcal{A}_c - \frac{1}{2} \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{d} \frac{(x_i - \mu_{c,i})^2}{\sigma_{c,i}^2}}_{m^2_c(\mathbf{x})}
\end{equation}
where $\ln \mathcal{A}_c = -\frac{d}{2}\ln(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2}\sum_i \ln(\sigma_{c,i}^2)$ is the log-normalisation constant and $m^2_c(\mathbf{x})$ is the squared Diag-Cov Mahalanobis distance. For truly Gaussian data this distance concentrates tightly around its expected value $d$ (see Section~\ref{sec:method:gaussianity}), but the \emph{difference} in log-likelihoods between the winning class and any other class grows with $d$. Intuitively, each of the $d=768$ standardised coordinates contributes a small increment to the distance gap, and these increments accumulate. When the raw log-likelihoods are converted into a posterior, even a modest advantage translates into a near-one-hot posterior: the probability mass collapses almost entirely onto the single best-fitting class. This \emph{uncertainty collapse} makes the raw posterior probabilities unreliable as confidence estimates, which is particularly harmful in continual learning, where the number of competing classes grows with each new task.

\paragraph{Bayesian Temperature Scaling}
To recover reliable uncertainty estimates without altering the MAP decision boundary, we introduce a Temperature Scaling parameter $T > 0$ directly into the Bayesian inference process. We treat $T$ as a global smoothing factor applied to the log-likelihoods before softmax normalisation. The calibrated posterior is:
\begin{equation}
	p(y=c \mid \mathbf{x};\, T) = \exp \!\left( \frac{1}{T} \log p(\mathbf{x} \mid y=c) - \mathcal{L}_E \right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_E$ is the log-evidence computed via the numerically stable logsumexp operator:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}_E = \operatorname{logsumexp}_{j \in \mathcal{Y}} \!\left( \frac{1}{T} \log p(\mathbf{x} \mid y=j) \right)
\end{equation}
Choosing $T > 1$ divides all log-likelihoods by $T$, shrinking the differences between classes proportionally and \emph{softening} the posterior distribution. Choosing $T < 1$ sharpens it. Crucially, $T$ does not change the rank order of the log-likelihoods, so the MAP prediction is preserved. The effect on uncertainty is captured by the calibration metrics defined in Section~\ref{sec:method:metrics}: a well-chosen $T$ reduces the Expected Calibration Error (ECE), lowers the Confidence on Errors (CoE), and brings the predictive entropy closer to the difficulty of the task.

\subsection{Gaussianity Tests}
\label{sec:method:gaussianity}

Before committing to a class-conditional Gaussian classifier we must verify that the assumption is empirically defensible on the DINOv2 embedding space.
We employ three complementary tests, arranged from weakest (moment-based) to strongest (formal hypothesis test) for the univariate case, plus a separate multivariate test.

\subsubsection{Per-Dimension Gaussianity: Moments and Formal Test}
\label{sec:method:gaussianity:moments}
\phantomsection\label{sec:method:gaussianity:normaltest}

To verify Gaussianity at the level of individual feature coordinates, we use three complementary indicators applied to every (class~$c$, dimension~$j$) pair across the 80\,\% training split.

\paragraph{Shape moments.}
Following the normality diagnostics of Pearson~\cite{pearson1895skewness} and D'Agostino and Pearson~\cite{dagostino1971omnibus,dagostino1973tests}, we compute the sample skewness and excess kurtosis for each class~$c$ along each of the $d=768$ coordinates:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{Skew}}_{c,j} \;=\; \frac{\frac{1}{n_c}\sum_{i}(x_{i,j}-\mu_{c,j})^3}{\hat\sigma_{c,j}^{3}},\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{Kurt}}_{c,j} \;=\; \frac{\frac{1}{n_c}\sum_{i}(x_{i,j}-\mu_{c,j})^4}{\hat\sigma_{c,j}^{4}} - 3,
\label{eq:moments}
\end{equation}
so that both quantities vanish exactly for a Gaussian~\cite{pearson1895skewness,joyce2011kurtosis}. We report their mean, standard deviation, mean absolute value, and the fraction of coordinates satisfying the informal cut-offs $|\mathrm{Skew}|<0.5$ and $|\mathrm{Kurt}|<1.0$.

\paragraph{D'Agostino--Pearson omnibus test.}
The moments alone do not constitute a formal statistical test. We therefore apply the D'Agostino--Pearson omnibus test to every (class, dimension) pair~\cite{dagostino1971omnibus,dagostino1973tests}. The test combines the two shape statistics into a single composite statistic:
\begin{equation}
K^2 \;=\; Z_1\!\left(\widehat{\mathrm{Skew}}\right)^2 + Z_2\!\left(\widehat{\mathrm{Kurt}}\right)^2 \;\sim\; \chi^2_2 \quad \text{under } H_0,
\label{eq:normaltest}
\end{equation}
where $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are normalised transformations of the skewness and kurtosis estimators respectively, and $H_0$ is the hypothesis that the data are normally distributed. We reject $H_0$ at $\alpha=0.05$ and report the fraction of pairs that \emph{pass} the test (i.e.\ fail to reject normality).

\paragraph{Relaxed criterion.}
Because the omnibus test is sensitive to the relatively large class sizes available ($n_c\!\approx\!80$--$400$)---even minor departures from perfect normality accumulate into significant $K^2$ values---we supplement it with a size-independent indicator: a dimension is classified as near-Gaussian if $|\mathrm{Skew}|<1$ \emph{and} $|\mathrm{Kurt}|<1$ hold simultaneously. The strict test and the relaxed criterion together provide a lower and an upper bound on the fraction of approximately Gaussian feature dimensions; both are reported in Section~\ref{sec:gaussianity_results}.


\subsubsection{Multivariate Gaussianity: Henze--Zirkler Test}
\label{sec:method:gaussianity:multivariate}

Per-dimension normality is necessary but not sufficient for the joint $d$-dimensional distribution to be Gaussian. A set of coordinates can each be marginally normal while the joint distribution departs from multivariate normality due to non-linear dependence or higher-order cross-moment structure. We therefore complement the univariate analysis with the Henze--Zirkler (HZ) test~\cite{henze1990class}, whose theoretical basis is described in Section~\ref{sec:method:gaussianity:hz_theory}.

\paragraph{Diagonal-covariance HZ at native resolution ($d=768$).}
As a first pass, the test is applied in the native $d=768$ feature space using a manual diagonal-covariance formulation. The Mahalanobis distances required by the HZ statistic are computed analytically by inverting only the $d$ per-coordinate sample variances (circumventing the rank-deficiency that arises from full matrix inversion when $n_c<d$), with a numerical floor of $\varepsilon=10^{-10}$ applied to guard against inactive dimensions. All available samples for each class are used (100\,\% of the data, no train/test split). Under exact multivariate normality, $\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathrm{HZ}}] = 1 - (1+2b^2)^{-d/2} \to 1$ for large $d$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:hz_expected}), so $T_{\mathrm{HZ}} \approx 1$ is the Gaussian reference at native dimensionality. The resulting $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}$ values, together with their per-dataset histograms, are reported in Section~\ref{sec:mv_gaussianity_results}.

\paragraph{PCA-reduced HZ (valid multivariate analysis).}
To obtain a statistically informative multivariate HZ test, we project each class to a $K$-dimensional PCA subspace where the sample covariance is full rank ($K = \min(\lfloor n_c/3 \rfloor, 50)$ ensures $n_c \geq 3K$). The test is then applied by \texttt{pingouin.multivariate\_normality}~\cite{vallat2018pingouin} in this reduced space. The bandwidth $b$ follows the standard HZ recommendation~\cite{henze1990class}, and the same 80\,\% training split as the univariate tests is used. Because $K \leq 50 \ll d$, the kernel terms no longer underflow and the resulting $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}$ values---close to~$1$ for Gaussian data and growing monotonically with distributional complexity---are reported together with per-dataset histograms in Section~\ref{sec:mv_gaussianity_results}.

\paragraph{Reported quantities (multivariate).}
For each benchmark and each analysis variant (diagonal $d=768$ and PCA-reduced), we report the mean and standard deviation of $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}$ across all classes, together with the $[\mathrm{P}_{10}, \mathrm{P}_{90}]$ percentile band.

\subsubsection{Univariate Henze--Zirkler Test per Dimension}
\label{sec:method:gaussianity:hz_univariate}

As a complement to both the moment-based diagnostics of Section~\ref{sec:method:gaussianity:moments} and the multivariate HZ test above, we introduce a univariate HZ analysis applied independently to every (class~$c$, dimension~$j$) pair. This bridges the two levels: it uses the same formal HZ statistic as the multivariate test but at $d=1$, providing a stricter hypothesis-based criterion than the D'Agostino--Pearson omnibus test while also being more interpretable than the full-dimensional multivariate result.

\paragraph{Bandwidth.}
For $d=1$ the standard HZ smoothing parameter reduces to:
\begin{equation}
b^2 \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{2/5} n_c^{-2/5},
\label{eq:hz_bw_univariate}
\end{equation}
which decays as $n_c^{-2/5}$ and is small ($b^2 \approx 0.04$) for the class sizes encountered here ($n_c \in [60,\,400]$).

\paragraph{Efficient vectorised computation.}
For $d=1$ the pairwise kernel term in $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}$ can be expanded via its Taylor series around $b^2 x_j x_k = 0$:
\begin{equation}
A_m \;=\; \frac{1}{n_c^2}\sum_{j,k} e^{-\frac{b^2}{2}(z_{j,m}-z_{k,m})^2}
      \;=\; \frac{1}{n_c^2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}
            \frac{b^{2\ell}}{\ell!}\,S_\ell^2\;+\;\mathcal{O}\!\left((b^2)^{L+1}\right),
\label{eq:hz_taylor}
\end{equation}
where $S_\ell = \sum_j v_{j,m}\,z_{j,m}^\ell$ and $v_{j,m} = \exp(-b^2 z_{j,m}^2 / 2)$. All $d=768$ dimensions are processed simultaneously; the memory footprint is $\mathcal{O}(n_c \cdot d)$ (no $n_c \times n_c$ matrix is formed). With $L=8$ terms the approximation error is below $10^{-6}$ for all encountered bandwidths, verified against the exact per-dimension computation.

\paragraph{Expected value under $H_0$ and p-value.}
Under exact normality the expected value of $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}$ for a $d$-dimensional problem is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_{H_0}[T_{\mathrm{HZ}}] \;=\; 1 - (1 + 2b^2)^{-d/2},
\label{eq:hz_expected}
\end{equation}
which unifies both the univariate and multivariate cases. For $d=1$ and the small bandwidths encountered here ($b^2\approx 0.04$), the term $(1+2b^2)^{-1/2}\approx 0.96$ remains close to~$1$, so $\mathbb{E}_{H_0}[T_{\mathrm{HZ}}]\approx 0.04$. For large $d$ (e.g.\ the $d=768$ multivariate case), even a modest bandwidth causes $(1+2b^2)^{-d/2}\to 0$, driving $\mathbb{E}_{H_0}[T_{\mathrm{HZ}}]\to 1$. In short, the Gaussian reference value is near~$0$ for $d=1$ and near~$1$ for large $d$; both cases follow from the same formula with different exponents. For the univariate analysis, with $d=1$, the expected value is approximately $0.04$ for $n_c=400$ and increases toward~$1$ as $n_c\to 0$ (fewer samples, wider kernel). Empirically, the ratio $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}\,/\,\mathbb{E}_{H_0}[T_{\mathrm{HZ}}]$ follows a $\chi^2(1)$ distribution under $H_0$ (verified by simulation with coefficient of variation $\approx\sqrt{2}$), yielding the one-tailed p-value:
\begin{equation}
p \;=\; 1 - F_{\chi^2(1)}\!\left(\frac{T_{\mathrm{HZ}}}{\mathbb{E}_{H_0}[T_{\mathrm{HZ}}]}\right).
\label{eq:hz_pvalue}
\end{equation}
A dimension $j$ of class $c$ passes the test if $p > \alpha = 0.05$, i.e.\ $T_{\mathrm{HZ}} < 3.841\,\mathbb{E}_{H_0}[T_{\mathrm{HZ}}]$.

\paragraph{Reported quantities (univariate HZ).}
For each benchmark we report the distribution of $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}$ values over all $(C \times d)$ pairs (histograms), the mean per-class $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}$, and the fraction of pairs passing the test at $\alpha=0.05$. Results are in Section~\ref{sec:uv_hz_gaussianity_results}.

\subsection{Inter-Class Pairwise Bhattacharyya Distance}
\label{sec:method:gaussianity:interclass}

While the Henze--Zirkler test of Section~\ref{sec:method:gaussianity:hz_theory} measures the goodness-of-fit of individual classes to the diagonal-Gaussian model, the \emph{inter-class} pairwise Bhattacharyya distance measures how distinguishable any two class-conditional Gaussians are from one another.
The Bhattacharyya distance is symmetric and provides a direct connection to the Bayes error rate via the Bhattacharyya coefficient $\rho_{ij} = e^{-D_B(i,j)}$.
Under the diagonal-Gaussian parameterisation, the Bhattacharyya distance between classes $i$ and $j$ is:
\begin{equation}
    D_B(i,j)
    = \frac{1}{8}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j)^{\top}
      \bar{\Sigma}^{-1}
      (\boldsymbol{\mu}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j)
      + \frac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(
        \frac{\det\bar{\Sigma}}{\sqrt{\det\Sigma_i\,\det\Sigma_j}}
      \right),
    \label{eq:interclass_bhattacharyya}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\Sigma} = (\Sigma_i + \Sigma_j)/2$.
Because both $\Sigma_i$ and $\bar{\Sigma}$ are diagonal, all matrix operations reduce to element-wise arithmetic: determinants become sums of logs, and the matrix inverse reduces to element-wise reciprocals.
$D_B=0$ indicates complete distributional overlap; larger values correspond to greater separability and a lower Bhattacharyya coefficient $\rho = e^{-D_B}$.
We track three statistics as the class count grows: (a)~the mean pairwise $D_B$ with a $[\mathrm{P}_{10},\mathrm{P}_{90}]$ percentile band, (b)~the minimum pairwise $D_B$ (the hardest class pair), and (c)~the count of pairs below selected threshold values to characterise the tail of the distribution.
Together, these three curves characterise the class-confusion landscape that the Bayesian classifier must navigate as the continual-learning stream progresses.

\subsection{Spectral Analysis of the Embedding Space}
\label{sec:method:gaussianity:pca}

To characterise the global structure of the DINOv2 feature space, independently of the class-conditional model, we compute the PCA spectrum of the $\ell_2$-normalised embeddings pooled across all training classes.
The $d=768$ eigenvalues of the empirical data covariance are sorted in decreasing order; we report both the individual percentage-of-variance contribution of each principal component and the cumulative explained variance.
This diagnostic measures the \emph{effective intrinsic dimensionality} of the shared representation: a rapid eigenvalue decay implies that the discriminative signal is concentrated in a low-dimensional subspace, whereas a slow decay indicates that the signal is distributed across the full ambient space.
The spectral profile also constrains the effective rank available to the per-class covariance estimators: a per-class estimator can only populate directions already covered by the pooled class spectrum.
Results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:spectral_analysis}.


\section{Generative-Discriminative Fusion and Re-ranking Strategies}

In high-dimensional classification tasks, relying on a single metric often fails to capture the full complexity of the feature space. While generative approaches (Mahalanobis-based) model the underlying class-conditional distributions, discriminative approaches (Cosine-based) excel at capturing angular separability on the unit hypersphere. To leverage the strengths of both, we evaluate several fusion and re-ranking strategies.

Importantly, these strategies are agnostic to the specific structure of the class-conditional distributions; they can be applied whether the underlying generative model utilizes a \textbf{diagonal covariance} matrix for computational efficiency or a \textbf{full covariance} matrix to capture complex feature correlations.

The nine strategies are presented in the order used throughout the rest of the thesis: the \emph{recommended} group (SoftTaskFusion, TaskMeanShrink, Top5-CosineReRank and OptFusion-MinMax) comes first; the \emph{brittle} group (HardGate, Top5-TaskReRank, TopK-TaskFilter) follows; and the two \emph{marginal} LDA variants (HardGate-LDA, SoftGate-LDA) close the section. The corresponding empirical taxonomy is derived from the analysis of the experimental results.


\subsection{SoftTaskFusion: Soft Class--Task Log-Probability Fusion} \label{df:softtaskfusion}

The Soft Task-Aware Fusion strategy mitigates the rigidity of hierarchical gating by treating the task assignment as a latent variable in a joint probability framework. In this approach, the final decision score for a class $c$ is determined by combining its class-conditional log-likelihood with the log-likelihood of its parent task $t(c)$. Mathematically, given a feature vector $\mathbf{x}$, the final score $S_c$ for each active class is defined as:
\begin{equation}
	S_c(\mathbf{x}) = \ln P(\mathbf{x}|c) + \ln P(\mathbf{x}|t(c))
\end{equation}
where $\ln P(\mathbf{x}|c)$ and $\ln P(\mathbf{x}|t(c))$ represent the Gaussian log-likelihoods at the class and task levels, respectively. These are computed as:
\begin{equation}
	\ln P(\mathbf{x}|i) = \ln A_i - \frac{1}{2}(x - \mu_i)^T \Sigma_i^{-1} (x - \mu_i), \quad i \in {c, t}
\end{equation}
During inference, the task-level log-likelihoods $\ln P(\mathbf{x}|t)$ are calculated for all tasks currently seen in the stream. These values are then ``expanded'' or broadcasted to all constituent classes within those tasks. The classification rule is given by:
\begin{equation}
	c^* = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{C}_{active}} \left[ S_c(\mathbf{x}) \right]
\end{equation}
By performing an additive fusion in the log-domain (equivalent to a product of probabilities), this method allows the model to ``boost'' the scores of classes whose parent task distribution highly matches the input, while still allowing a class to be selected even if its task-level likelihood is not the absolute maximum, provided the class-level evidence is sufficiently strong. This approach effectively balances global class competition with coarse task-level guidance.

\subsection{TaskMeanShrink: Task-Level MAP Mean Shrinkage} \label{df:taskmeanshrink}

To improve the robustness of the class-level generative models, we implement a Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimation for the mean vectors. This strategy, often referred to as Mean Shrinkage, addresses the high variance in class-specific estimates—especially when data is scarce—by anchoring them to a task-level prior.

In this framework, each class $c$ belonging to a task $t$ is modeled using a task-aware mean $\mu_{c, \text{MAP}}$. This mean is a convex combination of the empirical class mean $\hat{\mu}_c$ and the pooled task mean $\mu_t$:
\begin{equation}
	\mu_{c, \text{MAP}} = \frac{n_c \hat{\mu}_c + \lambda \mu_t}{n_c + \lambda}
\end{equation}
where $n_c$ is the number of samples in class $c$, and $\lambda$ is a shrinkage hyperparameter (set to $10.0$ in our experiments) that controls the influence of the task-level prior. The task mean $\mu_t$ is computed by pooling the features of all classes within task $t$:
\begin{equation}
	\mu_t = \frac{1}{\sum_{i \in \text{Task}_t} n_i} \sum_{i \in \text{Task}_t} \sum_{j=1}^{n_i} x_{i,j}
\end{equation}

By shrinking the class means toward the task centroid, the model reduces over-fitting to class-specific noise while emphasizing features that characterize the broader task domain. Once the MAP means are calculated, they are used to compute the Mahalanobis distance in the log-likelihood score:
\begin{equation}
	g_c(\mathbf{x}) = \ln A_c - \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c, \text{MAP}})^T \Sigma_c^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c, \text{MAP}})
\end{equation}
This approach provides a ``Bayesian'' regularization effect, ensuring that classes with very few samples or high intra-class variance are pull towards a more stable task-level representation, thereby improving generalizability in the global classification space.


\subsection{Top5-CosineReRank: Generative--Angular Cosine Re-ranking} \label{df:top5cosrerank}

The Generative-Angular Re-ranking strategy leverages both the statistical modeling of Mahalanobis-based distances and the angular separability of Cosine similarity. In high-dimensional latent spaces, particularly those generated by models trained with contrastive or foundation objectives like DINOv2, the direction of the feature vector often carries significant discriminative information that the Euclidean-based Mahalanobis distance might under-prioritize.

The inference procedure is structured as a two-stage pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Generative Candidate Filtering:} For an input feature $\mathbf{x}$, we first compute the log-likelihood scores across all active classes $\mathcal{C}_{active}$ using the Gaussian generative model (utilizing either diagonal or full covariance matrices):
	\begin{equation}
		g_c(\mathbf{x}) = \ln A_c - \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^T \Sigma_c^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)
	\end{equation}
	From these scores, a candidate set $\mathcal{K}$ containing the Top-5 classes with the highest likelihood is extracted.
	\item \textbf{Angular Re-ranking:} Within the subset $\mathcal{K}$, the final prediction $c^*$ is determined by maximizing the Cosine similarity between the input feature $\mathbf{x}$ and the class mean vectors $\mu_c$:
	\begin{equation}
		c^* = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{K}} \frac{\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}_c}{\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_c\|}
	\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

By using the Gaussian model as a broad filter and Cosine similarity as a fine-grained refiner, this approach ensures that the chosen class is not only statistically probable given the distribution volume but also geometrically aligned with the class centroid on the unit hypersphere. This hybrid approach is particularly effective in mitigating the ``over-confidence'' of Gaussian models in regions where class distributions overlap significantly.

\subsection{OptFusion-MinMax: Optimised Min--Max Score Fusion} \label{df:optfusionminmax}

The Optimized Score Fusion strategy seeks to integrate the probabilistic rigor of generative Gaussian modeling with the angular robustness of Cosine similarity. Because log-likelihoods from a high-dimensional Gaussian distribution and Cosine similarity scores operate on vastly different numerical scales and ranges, a direct combination is mathematically unsound. To address this, we implement a dynamic fusion pipeline utilizing Min-Max Normalization and automated hyperparameter optimization.

For a given input $\mathbf{x}$, we first compute two independent score vectors across all active classes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Generative Score ($S_{gen}$):} The Mahalanobis-based log-likelihood $g_c(\mathbf{x})$.
\item \textbf{Discriminative Score ($S_{dis}$):} The Cosine similarity between $x$ and the class mean $\mu_c$:
\begin{equation}
\text{cos}(\theta_c) = \frac{\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}_c}{\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_c\|}
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

To perform the fusion, both score vectors are independently mapped to the range $[0, 1]$ using a Min-Max normalization function:
\begin{equation}
\text{Norm}(S) = \frac{S - \min(S)}{\max(S) - \min(S) + \epsilon}
\end{equation}
The final decision score $S_{final}$ is then computed as a convex combination of the normalized vectors:
\begin{equation}
S_{final} = \alpha \cdot \text{Norm}(S_{gen}) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \text{Norm}(S_{dis})
\end{equation}



The fusion weight $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ is determined through an automated Bayesian hyperparameter search over a validation subset (20\% of the training data). By maximising the Average Accuracy on this validation set, the optimal balance between the generative and discriminative evidence is identified prior to the final evaluation on the held-out test set. This approach ensures that the fusion weight reflects the geometric properties of the DINOv2 feature space rather than a fixed heuristic.

\subsection{HardGate: Hierarchical Task Gating} \label{df:hardgate}

This strategy implements a strict two-step decision process. First, a global gating mechanism identifies the most probable task $t^*$ for a given input $\mathbf{x}$. Once the task is fixed, the final classification is restricted solely to the classes belonging to that task.The task-level distribution is modeled as a Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu_t, \Sigma_t)$, where the parameters are estimated by pooling all samples from the classes belonging to task $t$. The inference logic is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Task Selection:} $t^* = \arg\max_{t} \left( \ln A_t - \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_t)^T \Sigma_t^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_t) \right)$
\item \textbf{Local Classification:} $c^* = \arg\max_{c \in \text{Task}_{t^*}} \left( \ln A_c - \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^T \Sigma_c^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c) \right)$
\end{enumerate}
This approach significantly reduces the search space during inference but is highly sensitive to errors in the initial gating step (Task-IL vs. Class-IL interference).

\subsection{Top5-TaskReRank: Top-5 Candidate Re-ranking via Task Likelihood} \label{df:top5taskrerank}
Unlike the strict gating, this strategy performs an initial global search across all active classes but uses task-level information to refine the final choice among the most likely candidates.The process follows these steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Candidate Selection:} The model identifies the Top-5 classes with the highest log-likelihoods across the entire set of active classes.
\item \textbf{Task-Guided Verification:} For each candidate in the Top-5, the model retrieves the likelihood of the parent task $p(\mathbf{x}|t)$.
\item \textbf{Selection:} The final class $c^*$ is chosen as the one among the Top-5 candidates whose parent task yields the highest task-level likelihood.
\end{enumerate}
This strategy provides a ``safety net'' where the fine-grained class similarity is cross-referenced with the coarser task-level distribution to resolve ambiguities.

\subsection{TopK-TaskFilter: Top-$K$ Task Candidate Filtering} \label{df:topktaskfilter}

This strategy is a hybrid between strict gating and global search. It expands the search space of Gating by considering the $N$ most probable tasks (where $N=3$ in the implementation) and evaluating all classes within those selected tasks.The decision rule is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
	c^* = \arg\max_{c \in \bigcup \text{Top-}N(\text{Tasks})} \left( \ln A_c - \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^T \Sigma_c^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c) \right)
\end{equation}

By evaluating a ``neighborhood'' of likely tasks, this method mitigates the risk of a single incorrect gating decision while maintaining computational efficiency by ignoring tasks that are highly unlikely according to the pooled task-level Gaussian models.


\subsection{HardGate-LDA: Hierarchical LDA Subspace Inference} \label{df:hardgatelda}

This strategy combines global task gating with local expert models optimized via Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA). By projecting high-dimensional DINOv2 features ($d=768$) into a task-specific 9-dimensional subspace, the model maximizes the separability of classes within that task while reducing noise.

The process is defined as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Subspace Projection:} For each task $t$, we compute the within-class scatter matrix $S_w$ and the between-class scatter matrix $S_b$ using the classes belonging to that task. We solve the generalized eigenvalue problem $S_b v = \lambda S_w v$ to obtain the projection matrix $W_t \in \mathbb{R}^{768 \times 9}$, which contains the top 9 eigenvectors.
\item \textbf{Stage 1 (Hard Gating):} A test sample $\mathbf{x}$ is first assigned to a task $t^*$ by maximizing the log-likelihood of task-pooled Gaussian distributions in the original 768D space:
\begin{equation}
t^* = \arg\max_{t} \left( \ln A_t - \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_t)^T \Sigma_t^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_t) \right)
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Stage 2 (Local Subspace Classification):} The feature is projected into the specific subspace of task $t^*$: $\mathbf{x}' = W_{t^*}^T \mathbf{x}$. The final class is predicted using non-separable (full) covariance Gaussians modeled within this 9D space:
\begin{equation}
c^* = \arg\max_{c \in \text{Task}_{t^*}} \left( \ln A_{c, \text{proj}} - \frac{1}{2}(z' - \mu_{c, \text{proj}})^T \Sigma_{c, \text{proj}}^{-1} (z' - \mu_{c, \text{proj}}) \right)
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}



\subsection{SoftGate-LDA: Soft Hierarchical LDA Fusion} \label{df:softgatelda}

This other strategy improves upon the previous one by replacing the hard gating decision with a joint probabilistic fusion. This prevents a single error in the task selection stage from irrecoverably leading to a misclassification.

Instead of selecting $t^*$ and then calculating the class likelihood, SoftGate-LDA evaluates a total score for every class across all tasks. The decision rule combines the global gating evidence with the local subspace likelihood:
\begin{equation}
c^* = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{C}_{\text{active}}} \left( \text{Score}_{\text{gating}}(t(c)) + \text{Score}_{\text{LDA}}(c | W_{t(c)}) \right)
\end{equation}
where $t(c)$ is the task to which class $c$ belongs. For every class, the feature $\mathbf{x}$ is projected into the subspace associated with its task. The scores are computed as:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$\text{Score}_{\text{gating}}$:} The log-likelihood of the parent task in the original 768D space.
\item \textbf{$\text{Score}_{\text{LDA}}$:} The log-likelihood of the class in its task-specific 9D LDA subspace.
\end{itemize}
This additive fusion in the log-domain (equivalent to a product of probabilities) allows the local expert models to influence the task selection. If a class in Task A has an extremely high likelihood in its 9D subspace, it can override a slightly higher task-level likelihood for Task B, leading to more robust global classification.
\section{Sample Efficiency and Incremental Class Inclusion}
\label{sec:method:incremental}

To quantify how quickly each closed-form estimator converges as training data accumulates, we design an incremental class inclusion experiment.
Starting from a base classifier trained on nine classes, a tenth target class is introduced and its training samples are presented in successive steps of $10\,\%$ of the total per-class budget.
After each step, the updated classifier is evaluated on the held-out test set of all ten active classes and the accuracy on the new class is recorded.
Two representative target classes per benchmark are selected to span the range of class indices.
This protocol directly measures the \emph{sample efficiency} of each covariance estimator---how many samples are required before the classifier reaches its asymptotic accuracy---which is directly relevant to the continual-learning setting where new classes may arrive with limited data.
The corresponding results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:incremental_update}.

\section{Backbones Comparison}
\label{sec:method:backbones}

Throughout this work, DINOv2 \cite{oquab2024dinov} is used as the default backbone feature extractor for the Gaussian-based classifiers because of its self-supervised training regime and the strong empirical evidence of approximately Gaussian, well-conditioned class-conditional distributions presented in Section~\ref{sec:method:gaussianity}. However, the proposed framework is agnostic to the specific feature extractor used: any frozen encoder that produces fixed-dimensional embeddings can plug in unchanged into the downstream Gaussian classifier. To quantify the dependence of the framework on the choice of backbone, we further analyse the impact of the backbone architecture by re-running the Diag-Cov and Full-Cov variants using two supervised Vision Transformers from the \texttt{timm} library \cite{rw2019timm}: \textbf{ViT-base/16} (ViT-b, $d=768$) and \textbf{ViT-medium/16} (ViT-m, $d=512$), both pre-trained on ImageNet-21k \cite{imagenet21k}. The covariance estimation, hyper-parameters and evaluation protocol are kept identical across backbones to isolate the effect of the upstream representation; the results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:backbone_results}.

\label{sec:method:calibration_gaussianity}

\subsection{Confidence and Calibration Metrics}

While accuracy and forgetting measure the correctness of predictions, they do not account for the model's reliability or its tendency to over-confide in incorrect decisions---a common issue in Class-Incremental Learning. To address this, we introduce five complementary metrics that jointly characterise the probabilistic behaviour of the classifier: the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) measures the global alignment between confidence and accuracy; the Confidence on Errors (CoE) quantifies how dangerously overconfident the model is when it fails; Total Confidence (TC) and Probability Margin (PM) both capture the decisiveness of the posterior distribution from two complementary angles; and the Average Predictive Entropy ($H$) measures the spread of probability mass across all classes. All five are defined below in the order in which they appear in Table~\ref{tab:metrics_summary}.

\subsubsection{Expected Calibration Error (ECE)}
This metric quantifies the mismatch between predictive confidence and empirical accuracy. We partition the predictions into $M$ equally spaced bins based on their confidence scores. The ECE is defined as the weighted average of the absolute difference between the accuracy and confidence of each bin:

\begin{equation}
	ECE_t = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{N} \left| acc(B_m) - conf(B_m) \right|\label{eq_ECE}
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the total number of samples, $B_m$ is the set of indices of samples whose prediction confidence falls into the $m$-th interval, and $acc(B_m)$ and $conf(B_m)$ represent the mean accuracy and mean confidence within that bin, respectively.

\subsubsection{Confidence on Errors (CoE)}
 To specifically analyze the model's behavior when it fails, we measure the average confidence assigned to the winning (but incorrect) class. High CoE indicates a model that is ``dangerously wrong'', which is a critical factor in safety-oriented applications. It is computed for a task $t$ as:

\begin{equation}
	CoE_t = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_t|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{E}_t} \max(\hat{p}_i)
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{E}_t$ is the set of misclassified samples and $\hat{p}_i$ is the predicted probability distribution for sample $i$.

\subsubsection{Total Confidence (TC)}

Total Confidence measures how certain the model is on average about its top-1 prediction, regardless of whether that prediction is correct:

\begin{equation}
	TC_t = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \max_{c} \, p_{i,c}
\end{equation}

A value of $TC=1$ means the model assigns all probability mass to a single class for every sample---it is always maximally decisive. A value close to $1/C$ would indicate near-uniform posteriors. TC is a measure of \emph{decisiveness}, not correctness: a well-calibrated model should have $TC \approx A_T$ (confident when right, uncertain when wrong), whereas a pathologically overconfident model can reach $TC \approx 1$ even at low accuracy.

\subsubsection{Probability Margin (PM)}

The Probability Margin is the natural, non-logarithmic measure of how decisively the model separates its top-1 prediction from its second-best alternative:

\begin{equation}
	PM_t = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Bigl( p_{i,(1)} - p_{i,(2)} \Bigr)
\end{equation}

where $p_{i,(1)} \ge p_{i,(2)}$ are the two highest posterior probabilities for sample $i$. A margin near $1$ means the model almost entirely ignores its second candidate; a margin near $0$ means the model is nearly tied between its top-two guesses and one should treat the prediction with caution. PM is bounded in $[0,1]$ and directly interpretable: a value of $0.7$ means the winning class is, on average, assigned $70\%$ more probability than the runner-up.

\subsubsection{Average Predictive Entropy ($H$)}
We utilize Shannon entropy to measure the uncertainty of the model's predictions. A higher entropy suggests that the model is distributing its probability mass across multiple classes, indicating lower certainty. For task $t$, the average entropy is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
	H_t = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( - \sum_{c=1}^{C} p_{i,c} \log(p_{i,c}) \right)
\end{equation}

where $p_{i,c}$ is the probability assigned to class $c$ for sample $i$.
