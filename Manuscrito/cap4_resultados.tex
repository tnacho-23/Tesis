\chapter{Results}

This chapter reports the experimental evaluation of the proposed framework. The presentation is structured to follow a top-down validation of the modeling pipeline: \textbf{Section \ref{sec:gaussianity_results}} first inspects the per-dimension moments of the DINOv2 embeddings to verify that the Gaussian class-conditional assumption is a reasonable working hypothesis; \textbf{Section \ref{sec:covariance_results}} reports the core continual-learning metrics ($A_{10}$, $F_{10}$ and $A_{30}$, $F_{30}$) across our family of covariance estimators and mixture-of-Gaussian variants; \textbf{Section \ref{sec:df_results}} studies the generative-discriminative fusion and the score-normalization strategies that turn the Bayesian classifier into a competitive system; \textbf{Section \ref{sec:backbone_results}} ablates the impact of the feature extractor; and \textbf{Section \ref{sec:calibration_results}} closes the chapter with an analysis of the predictive confidence and the Expected Calibration Error.

For every benchmark we report the average accuracy after the last task, $A_{T}$, and the forgetting score $F_{T}$ as defined in Chapter \ref{methodolohy}. Whenever possible we report mean and variance over three independent runs (different task-order seeds); deterministic methods exhibit zero variance because the underlying Gaussian estimators are closed-form. State-of-the-art prompt-based competitors are reproduced from the published values reported in their respective papers. Methods proposed in this thesis are highlighted in grey.

\section{Gaussianity Analysis}
\label{sec:gaussianity_results}

The architecture proposed in Chapter \ref{methodolohy} models each class as a multivariate Gaussian in the frozen DINOv2 embedding space. The validity of this assumption is a prerequisite for the discriminant functions derived in Sections 3.2.1--3.2.3. To probe how close the empirical class-conditional features are to a normal distribution, we compute the sample skewness and excess kurtosis along each of the $d=768$ coordinates and for every class in the three datasets considered in this work; the resulting pool aggregates $\sum_c d$ univariate measurements. Skewness quantifies the asymmetry of the marginal distribution and kurtosis its tailedness, with both moments vanishing for a perfectly Gaussian variable \cite{dagostino1971omnibus,dagostino1973tests}.

Table \ref{tab:gaussianity_stats} reports the aggregate statistics. For CIFAR-100 the marginal distributions are remarkably well-behaved: 96.1\,\% of the dimensions exhibit an absolute skewness below $0.5$ and 98.2\,\% of them lie within $|\text{Kurt}|<1$, which is the threshold commonly cited as evidence of approximate normality \cite{Bishop2006}. The behaviour mildly deteriorates on ImageNet-R---a corpus that intentionally mixes artistic, sketch and natural renditions \cite{imagenetR}---where the dispersion of both moments roughly doubles, but more than 85\,\% of the coordinates still pass the same cut-off. Caltech-256 \cite{caltech256} represents the most demanding scenario: only 74.7\,\% of the dimensions are nearly symmetric and the average excess kurtosis approaches $0.66$, which is consistent with its high intra-class visual variability and the heavy-tailed feature responses produced by the foundation model. Importantly, on all datasets the mean skewness is essentially zero, indicating that the asymmetries are equally distributed between left and right tails---a key property that justifies estimating the class prototype via the empirical mean. The contrast between the \textit{raw} and \textit{norm} rows on CIFAR-100 further shows that $\ell_2$ normalisation has a negligible effect on the marginal moments, which agrees with the geometric argument given in Chapter \ref{methodolohy} that the DINOv2 representations are already approximately isotropic.

Figures \ref{fig:skewness_histograms} and \ref{fig:_histograms} display the full empirical distribution of both moments. The histograms are sharply peaked around zero on CIFAR-100, mildly broadened on ImageNet-R, and visibly heavier-tailed on Caltech-256, in agreement with the numerical summaries in Table \ref{tab:gaussianity_stats}. Taken together, these results support the use of a Gaussian class-conditional model as a faithful first-order approximation of the DINOv2 feature distributions, while also signalling that mixture-of-Gaussian or hybrid covariance variants are likely to pay off in the higher-variability datasets---a hypothesis we revisit in Section \ref{sec:covariance_results}.

Several observations follow. First, on CIFAR-100 the best of our methods (MOG4-H, 88.88\,\%) closes the gap with the strongest prompt-based system (RainbowPrompt, 89.86\,\%) to within one accuracy point, while requiring \emph{no backpropagation} and being deterministic by construction. Second, on ImageNet-R the diagonal estimator alone reaches 77.12\,\%, which is competitive with CPrompt and only $\sim2$ points below APT despite the dramatic domain shift; the gap is closed further by Shared-Full-Cov (77.06\,\%) at a much lower forgetting (3.97\,\%). Third, on Caltech-256 the hybrid and MOG4-F estimators dominate every prompt-based competitor, reaching 94.4\,\%; this is the regime where the data scarcity per class makes shrinkage and mixture modelling especially valuable, in agreement with the higher kurtosis values measured in Section \ref{sec:gaussianity_results}.

The forgetting columns show that our framework produces low and stable degradation: the closed-form estimators are typically below 5\,\% and Caltech-256 stays around 2\,\%, even though the model has no rehearsal mechanism. The variances reported for prompt-based competitors arise from stochastic training; in our case the variance reflects the random task split rather than parameter optimisation.

The Split Joint Datasets benchmark (Table \ref{tab:split_joint}) stress-tests the model on a 30-task stream that combines the three datasets in randomised order, with dramatic intra- and inter-task distribution shifts. The Hybrid-Cov estimator achieves the best trade-off (83.42\,\% / 3.86\,\%) thanks to its ability to retain feature correlations through the Mahalanobis term while sidestepping the numerical instability of the full log-determinant. The collapse of MOG4-F and MOG4-H in this scenario is consistent with the increased Caltech-256-driven kurtosis reported in Table \ref{tab:gaussianity_stats}: estimating four full-covariance components per class becomes ill-posed when the number of samples per component is small, and the resulting posterior is dominated by ill-conditioned eigenvalues. This is the regime where shrinkage and hybrid covariances visibly outperform purely full-rank estimators.

Three patterns emerge consistently across all four benchmarks. (i) Plain log-likelihood normalisation by the dimension (\textit{A1}--\textit{A3}) is too aggressive in the diagonal branch, where the determinant volume is small, and consistently destroys accuracy with respect to the unnormalised classifier; the same strategies are competitive in the full-covariance branch where the volume term is more informative. (ii) The Gemini variants behave similarly to A1/A2 and are dominated by the simpler \textit{B}/\textit{C} baselines that re-rank candidate classes by the unscaled log-density. (iii) Two strategies dominate: \textbf{LikelihoodCos}, which multiplicatively combines the log-likelihood with a cosine-similarity prior, and \textbf{MahalanobisT}, which applies a temperature-scaled Mahalanobis term. LikelihoodCos delivers the best $A_{10}$ on three of the four benchmarks (CIFAR-100, Caltech-256, and Joint with full covariance), often improving the unfused baseline by 0.5--1.0 accuracy points while keeping forgetting unchanged. MahalanobisT shines on lower forgetting, suggesting that temperature scaling has the expected smoothing effect on the posterior. These two strategies are the ones promoted to the calibration analysis of Section \ref{sec:calibration_results}.

\section{Model Confidence and Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration_results}

The accuracy and forgetting metrics evaluate the quality of the predicted label but say nothing about the reliability of the associated confidence. As detailed in Chapter \ref{methodolohy}, we measure this via the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) \cite{naeini2015obtaining,guo2017calibration} computed after every task, and we summarise the curve by its average value across the $T$ checkpoints, $\overline{\text{ECE}}$. Table \ref{tab:ece} reports the per-benchmark $\overline{\text{ECE}}$ for the diagonal and full-covariance branches.

Three findings stand out. First, the diagonal estimator is systematically better calibrated than the full-covariance one on every benchmark, and the gap grows with the difficulty of the stream---from a marginal 0.2 points on CIFAR-100 to almost 10 points on ImageNet-R and 26 points on the Joint benchmark. This is a direct consequence of the ill-conditioned log-determinant in high dimensions: the full-rank likelihoods are dominated by extreme volume contributions that bias the softmax-like posterior towards a handful of classes and produce overconfident, miscalibrated predictions. Second, on the long-horizon Joint benchmark the full-covariance $\overline{\text{ECE}}$ of 39.6\,\% reveals that the model becomes \emph{severely} overconfident as classes accumulate; the diagonal model contains the degradation at 13.5\,\%. Third, the absolute ECE values on CIFAR-100 and Caltech-256 (under 8\,\%) are competitive with the levels reported in the original temperature-scaling literature for ResNets and DenseNets on similar datasets \cite{guo2017calibration}, despite our model receiving no gradient signal.

The combination of these results with Section \ref{sec:df_results} motivates the use of the \textit{MahalanobisT} re-ranker as a default calibration mechanism: it inherits the strong accuracy of the full-covariance classifier while exposing a temperature parameter that can be optimised on a small held-out split, directly minimising the ECE without modifying the underlying Bayesian decision boundary. A more detailed analysis of the calibration curves---reliability diagrams, per-task ECE evolution, log-gap statistics and entropy distributions---is provided in the discussion chapter.
