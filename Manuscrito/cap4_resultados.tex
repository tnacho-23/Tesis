\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter presents the quantitative results of the experimental campaign in the following order: Section~\ref{sec:Gaussianity characterization_combined} characterises the feature space, consolidating the Gaussian parameterisation diagnostics, marginal and multivariate Gaussianity tests (Section~\ref{sec:gaussianity_results}), inter-class separability via pairwise Bhattacharyya distance (Section~\ref{sec:interclass_results}), and the PCA spectral decay of the DINOv2 embeddings together with the accuracy under PCA-reduced representations (Section~\ref{sec:spectral_analysis}); Section~\ref{sec:covariance_results} reports continual-learning metrics for the covariance estimators and mixture-of-Gaussian variants; Section~\ref{sec:df_results} covers the generative--discriminative re-ranking strategies; Section~\ref{sec:incremental_update} presents the sample efficiency and incremental class-inclusion curves; Section~\ref{sec:backbone_results} ablates the choice of feature extractor; and Section~\ref{sec:calibration_results_section} reports the probabilistic confidence and calibration metrics. All interpretation is deferred to Chapter~\ref{ch:analysis}.

For every benchmark we report the average accuracy after the last task, $A_{T}$, and the forgetting score $F_{T}$ as defined in Chapter~\ref{methodology}. Whenever possible we report mean and variance over three independent runs (different task-order seeds). Since the proposed Gaussian classifiers maintain only sufficient statistics $(\boldsymbol{\mu}_c, \Sigma_c)$ and do not involve parameter optimisation during incremental updates, the resulting classifier is independent of the specific class-presentation order; this explains the $\pm 0.00$ standard deviation reported for our methods throughout. State-of-the-art prompt-based competitors are reproduced from the published values reported in their respective papers. Methods proposed in this thesis are highlighted in grey. The best result per column is shown in bold.

A further structural property deserves attention before examining the tables. The proposed framework does not exhibit forgetting in the conventional sense of parameter overwriting: previously learned class statistics are never modified when new classes are introduced. The forgetting values reported here therefore reflect \emph{statistical interference}---the increased difficulty of the multi-class decision as the number of competing class prototypes grows---rather than degradation of previously learned representations.

\subsection{Score Components Distributions}
The diagonal-covariance Gaussian classifier assigns each test sample $\mathbf{x}$ to the class that maximizes the log-likelihood score:
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{score}(\mathbf{x}, c)
	= \underbrace{\ln A_c}_{\text{(a) volume}} \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,
	\underbrace{d_{\mathrm{mah}}(\mathbf{x},c)}_{\text{(b) shape}},
	\label{eq:score_components}
\end{equation}
where the two components are
\begin{align}
	\ln A_c &= -\tfrac{D}{2}\ln(2\pi) - \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{d=1}^{D}\ln\sigma^2_{c,d},
	\label{eq:lnA}\\
	d_{\mathrm{mah}}(\mathbf{x},c) &= \sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{(x_d - \mu_{c,d})^2}{\sigma^2_{c,d}}.
	\label{eq:dmah}
\end{align}
$\ln A_c$ depends only on the class variances (volume term); $d_{\mathrm{mah}}$ depends on the distance from the sample to the class mean weighted by the inverse variances (shape term). Figures~\ref{fig:hist_cifar_train}--\ref{fig:hist_imagenetr_val} show the empirical distributions of all three quantities, computed separately on the \textbf{training set} (parameters estimated from it) and the held-out \textbf{validation set} (20\,\%).

Two complementary per-feature normality assessments are reported in Table~\ref{tab:gaussianity_normaltest}.
The D'Agostino--Pearson normaltest at $\alpha=0.05$ provides the strictest formal criterion; it combines standardised skewness and kurtosis into a $\chi^2_2$ statistic and is therefore more sensitive than inspecting either moment alone.
The relaxed criterion---$|\mathrm{Skew}|<1$ \emph{and} $|\mathrm{Kurt}|<1$ simultaneously---is a lenient but transparent indicator of near-Gaussianity.
With training splits of $\bar{n}_c\approx400$, $120$ and $90$ samples per class for CIFAR-100, ImageNet-R and Caltech-256 respectively, even minor deviations from perfect normality are detectable, so the normaltest is conservative.
Nevertheless, $70$--$81\,\%$ of all (class, dimension) pairs pass the strict test, and the median $p$-value lies well above $0.05$ on every benchmark.
The relaxed criterion confirms that $82$--$98\,\%$ of pairs are near-Gaussian in both shape moments simultaneously.
Together with Table~\ref{tab:gaussianity_stats}, these results consistently support the marginal-Gaussianity assumption underlying the Diag-Cov classifier.

\subsubsection{Multivariate Gaussianity}
\label{sec:mv_gaussianity_results}


Table~\ref{tab:gaussianity_hz} reports the per-class Henze--Zirkler (HZ) test applied directly in the native $d=768$ feature space using an explicit diagonal covariance formulation. Rather than delegating to a library that internally constructs a full sample covariance matrix---which is rank-deficient whenever $n_c < d$ and yields degenerate results---the statistic is computed analytically by inverting only the $d$ per-coordinate sample variances (Section~\ref{sec:method:gaussianity:hz_theory}). All available samples for each class are used (100\,\% of the data, no train/test split), and a numerical floor of $\epsilon=10^{-10}$ is applied to each variance to guard against inactive dimensions.

\paragraph{How to read $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}$.}
The HZ statistic $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}$ measures how closely the empirical distribution matches a multivariate Gaussian by comparing their characteristic functions via pairwise Gaussian kernel distances. Under perfect multivariate normality, $T_{\mathrm{HZ}} = 1$ exactly. Values above~$1$ indicate that the data is \emph{more structured} (tighter clusters or heavier tails) than a Gaussian; values below~$1$ would indicate a flatter-than-Gaussian distribution. The farther $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}$ departs from~$1$, the stronger the departure from joint normality.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The observed class-averaged statistics are $1.0002$, $1.0033$, and $1.0016$ for CIFAR-100, ImageNet-R, and Caltech-256 respectively---all within $0.004$ of the Gaussian reference value of~$1$. This near-perfect alignment holds across the full $d=768$ space without any dimensionality reduction, demonstrating that the class-conditional diagonal-Gaussian model accurately describes the DINOv2 feature distributions at their native resolution. The small spread (std $\leq 0.009$) and tight percentile bands confirm that near-Gaussianity is a consistent property across virtually every class in all three benchmarks, not merely an artefact of averaging. The monotonic increase with dataset difficulty (ImageNet-R shows the highest mean and std) is consistent with the pattern in the univariate kurtosis analysis (Table~\ref{tab:gaussianity_stats}): DINOv2 embeddings are most Gaussian on CIFAR-100, where classes are visually compact, and exhibit slightly greater distributional complexity on the more diverse benchmarks. Taken together, these results provide strong multivariate confirmation that the diagonal-Gaussian generative classifier is a well-specified model for the feature distributions produced by the DINOv2 backbone.

\subsection{Inter-Class Pairwise Bhattacharyya Distance}
\label{sec:interclass_results}

Under the diagonal-covariance Gaussian model each class $c$ is represented by
$\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_c, \Sigma_c)$ with $\Sigma_c = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2_{c,1},\ldots,\sigma^2_{c,D})$.
The Bhattacharyya distance between two such distributions is

\begin{equation}
	D_B(i,j) =
	\frac{1}{8}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j)^{\top}
	\bar{\Sigma}^{-1}
	(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j)
	+
	\frac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(
	\frac{\det\bar{\Sigma}}{\sqrt{\det\Sigma_i\,\det\Sigma_j}}
	\right),
	\label{eq:bhattacharyya}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\bar{\Sigma} = (\Sigma_i + \Sigma_j)/2$.
Because both $\Sigma_i$ and $\bar{\Sigma}$ are diagonal, all matrix operations
reduce to element-wise arithmetic: $\det\Sigma = \prod_d \sigma^2_d$,
$\bar{\Sigma}^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(2/(\sigma^2_{i,d}+\sigma^2_{j,d}))$, and
log-determinants become sums of logs.
This avoids costly dense matrix inversions and keeps computation in $\mathcal{O}(C \cdot D)$
memory per class pair.
$D_B = 0$ indicates complete distributional overlap; larger values correspond to
greater separability and a lower Bhattacharyya coefficient $\rho = e^{-D_B}$.

Figure~\ref{fig:bhattacharyya} characterises the inter-class separability as the
class set grows from $k=2$ to the full benchmark size.
The mean pairwise $D_B$ increases markedly with benchmark scale:
CIFAR-100 ($89.43$, $[\mathrm{P}_{10}{=}56.85,\,\mathrm{P}_{90}{=}126.30]$),
ImageNet-R ($84.81$, $[\mathrm{P}_{10}{=}49.45,\,\mathrm{P}_{90}{=}122.85]$),
and Caltech-256 ($220.61$, $[\mathrm{P}_{10}{=}113.63,\,\mathrm{P}_{90}{=}350.79]$).
These large values confirm that the Diag-Cov Gaussian prototypes are
well-separated on average across all three benchmarks.
The minimum pairwise $D_B$---the hardest class pair for the classifier---reaches
$3.57$ on CIFAR-100, $11.54$ on ImageNet-R, and $20.96$ on Caltech-256
at the full class-set size, identifying the small fraction of near-confusable
prototypes that concentrate the residual classification error in the
continual-learning stream.

Pairs with $D_B < 20$  account for only $0.20\,\%$
($20/9\,900$) of CIFAR-100 pairs, $0.12\,\%$ ($48/39\,800$) of ImageNet-R
pairs, and $0.00\,\%$ of Caltech-256 pairs.
Broadening the threshold to $D_B < 50$  captures
$4.77\,\%$, $10.40\,\%$, and $0.15\,\%$ of pairs respectively.
Caltech-256's larger absolute distances reflect both its wider visual diversity
and the longer tail of its per-class sample distribution, which yields tighter,
less-overlapping Gaussian estimates.

Three patterns emerge from Table~\ref{tab:pca_accuracy}. First, increasing PCA dimensionality from 64 to 256 consistently improves diagonal-covariance accuracy ($+6.7$, $+7.8$, and $+2.8$ pp for CIFAR-100, ImageNet-R, and Caltech-256 respectively), confirming that 64 components discard discriminative variance retained by 256, as expected from the spectral decay in Figure~\ref{fig:pca_eigenvalues}. Second, the full covariance estimator outperforms the diagonal only when $n_c \gg d$: on CIFAR-100 at $d=64$ ($n_c \approx 400$) it gains $+4.6$ pp, but at $d=256$ the margin collapses to $-2.9$ pp as $n_c/d$ drops to $\approx\!1.6$. Third, on ImageNet-R and Caltech-256, where per-class counts range from $\approx\!80$ to $\approx\!230$, the full covariance degrades sharply at $d=64$ (by $14.1$ and $43.1$ pp respectively) but recovers at $d=256$ once the ratio $n_c/d$ becomes more favourable. These results confirm the well-known bias--variance trade-off: the diagonal assumption reduces estimation variance at the cost of a modelling bias that is only justified when sample budgets are limited relative to dimensionality.



\section{Covariance Estimation and Mixtures of Gaussians}

\section{Sample Efficiency and Incremental Class Inclusion}
\label{sec:incremental_update}

Figure~\ref{fig:incremental_class_inclusion} illustrates how the Diag-Cov and Full-Cov class-conditional models behave when samples of a new class arrive progressively. After learning an initial set of nine base classes, an additional class is introduced and its training samples are incorporated in steps of $10\,\%$ of the available data. Two representative target classes per benchmark are reported, chosen to span the dataset's class index range. On Split CIFAR-100 (\textbf{a}), the Diag-Cov classifier reaches a strong plateau already with $10\,\%$ of the samples (reflecting the small number of parameters required to estimate $d=768$ diagonal variances), while Full-Cov improves more gradually and surpasses Diag-Cov once 40--70\,\% of the samples are available. On Split ImageNet-R (\textbf{b}), the limited number of training samples per class (${\sim}40$ after the train/test split for some classes) makes the Full-Cov estimator saturate early at a lower-than-CIFAR ceiling; both estimators improve gradually but the gap between Diag-Cov and Full-Cov stays small. On Split Caltech-256 (\textbf{c}), Full-Cov already dominates from the very first $10\,\%$ slice---reflecting the higher visual variability that benefits more from inter-coordinate correlations---and Diag-Cov tracks the Full-Cov curve at a 1--2 point lower plateau.

\section{Confidence and Calibration Metrics}
\label{sec:calibration_results_section}

Table~\ref{tab:calibration_metrics} reports six metrics that characterise the probabilistic behaviour of the Gaussian classifier: the final-task accuracy ($A_T$ in \%), the Expected Calibration Error (ECE, \%), the Confidence on Errors (CoE), Total Confidence (TC), Probability Margin (PM), and the Predictive Entropy. All quantities are computed at the last after-task checkpoint and averaged across the per-task entries of that row. The original CIFAR-100 source files for Diag-Cov and Full-Cov in the data folder were byte-identical (a copy-paste error); we regenerated the Full-Cov metrics from the cached DINOv2 features so the two columns now reflect distinct estimators.

Two observations are worth noting for the Diag-Cov rows. First, TC and PM are systematically different: TC measures the raw top-1 probability while PM measures the \emph{margin} between the top-1 and top-2 probabilities. The gap between them ($\mathrm{TC}-\mathrm{PM} \approx 0.02$--$0.06$ depending on the dataset) represents the average probability mass placed on the runner-up class. On CIFAR-100, for instance, TC$=0.836$ but PM$=0.774$: the model is 83.6\% confident in its top choice, yet only 77.4\% more confident than its second choice, meaning the runner-up class retains roughly 6\% probability on average. This gap is largest on ImageNet-R and CIFAR-100---datasets with more visual ambiguity---and smallest on Caltech-256, where higher visual distinctiveness leaves little probability on the runner-up. Second, both TC and PM are substantially higher than the corresponding accuracy, confirming the overconfidence phenomenon discussed in Section~\ref{sec:method:highdimdim}.
