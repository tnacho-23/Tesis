\chapter{Experimental Results and Analysis}
\label{ch:results}
\label{ch:analysis}

This chapter presents the quantitative results of the experimental campaign together with their interpretation, topic by topic. Each section below first reports the relevant measurements and then, immediately afterwards, discusses the structural reasons behind the observed behaviour, so that evidence and analysis can be read side by side. The order of the sections mirrors the order in which the corresponding topics are introduced in the methodology (Chapter~\ref{methodology}). Section~\ref{sec:an:datasets} characterises the four benchmarks used throughout the thesis, including the empirical Gaussianity statistics that later sections validate formally. Section~\ref{sec:covariance_results} reports continual-learning metrics for the covariance estimators and mixture-of-Gaussian variants against prompt-based and analytic state-of-the-art baselines, together with the volume--shape trade-off that explains their relative ordering, the numerical correction of the Full-Cov and Hybrid-Cov estimators and its empirical consequences, the effect of the log-normalisation constant, the score-component distributions, linear shrinkage, mixture models, and the framework's positioning against prompt-based baselines. Section~\ref{sec:calibration_results_section} reports the probabilistic confidence and calibration metrics and interprets them as a further quality of the volume term. Section~\ref{sec:incremental_update} examines how average accuracy evolves as the pool of active classes is incrementally expanded. Section~\ref{sec:interclass_results} quantifies inter-class separability via the pairwise Bhattacharyya distance and interprets the resulting separability curves. Section~\ref{sec:backbone_results} ablates the choice of feature extractor and discusses the framework's sensitivity to this choice. Section~\ref{sec:spectral_analysis} characterises the PCA spectral decay of the DINOv2 embedding space and its consequence for PCA-reduced continual-learning accuracy. Section~\ref{sec:gaussianity_results} reports the statistical validation of the Gaussianity assumption, combining moment-based diagnostics with the formal D'Agostino--Pearson and Henze--Zirkler hypothesis tests, and interprets what this validation implies for the modelling choices made throughout. Section~\ref{sec:an:summary} closes the chapter by synthesising the principal findings into practical recommendations.

For every benchmark we report the average accuracy after the last task, $A_{T}$, and the forgetting score $F_{T}$ as defined in Chapter~\ref{methodology}. Whenever possible we report mean and variance over three independent runs (different task-order seeds). Since the proposed Gaussian classifiers maintain only sufficient statistics $(\boldsymbol{\mu}_c, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c)$ and do not involve parameter optimisation during incremental updates, the resulting classifier is independent of the specific class-presentation order; this explains the $\pm 0.00$ standard deviation reported for our methods throughout. State-of-the-art prompt-based competitors are reproduced from the published values reported in their respective papers. Methods proposed in this thesis are highlighted in grey. The best result per column is shown in bold.

The discussion that follows uses the principal results of Tables~\ref{tab:combined_benchmarks} and~\ref{tab:split_joint} to comprehensively evaluate the behavior of the alternative covariance parameterisations (Diag, Full, Hybrid, OAS, Ledoit--Wolf, Shared) and contrast them against the mixture-of-Gaussian variants.

\subsection{Covariance Parameterisations and the Shape-Volume Axis}
\label{sec:an:covariance_mechanics}

The performance hierarchy observed across the benchmarks is heavily governed by the ratio of the per-class sample budget $n_c$ to the feature dimensionality $d=768$, reflecting a distinct trade-off between structural capacity and numerical stability.

In the relatively high-sample regime of Split CIFAR-100 ($n_c = 400$), estimators that capture full off-diagonal correlations dominate. Both Full-Cov and MOG4-F match the state-of-the-art prompt-based method \textit{ConvPrompt} at $88.87\%$ without relying on any trainable parameters or upstream gradient optimization. This indicates that when the sample budget is deep enough to reliably approximate the $d^2$ free parameters of a covariance matrix, modeling the true geometric \emph{shape} of the class manifold provides a highly effective inductive bias that Euclidean or simple prototype-based models fail to capture.

Conversely, the limitations of unconstrained second-order modeling become obvious in severely under-sampled or imbalanced regimes. On Split ImageNet-R ($n_c = 40$), the empirical sample covariance matrix becomes highly singular, causing near-zero eigenvalues to corrupt the log-determinant volume term during Cholesky inversion. Under these exact constraints, the Hybrid-Cov estimator provides a highly robust fallback. By utilizing the full empirical structure exclusively for the Mahalanobis distance (the shape term) while replacing the sensitive log-determinant calculation with a well-conditioned diagonal volume approximation, it successfully preserves cross-feature correlations without succumbing to the spectral collapse that degrades standard full-covariance formulations.

\subsection{Linear Shrinkage versus Structural Decoupling}
\label{sec:an:shrinkage}

A critical finding of this evaluation is the persistent underperformance of classical linear shrinkage estimators, with OAS and Ledoit--Wolf (LW) achieving the lowest accuracy among the Gaussian-based methodologies across all constituent datasets. Linear shrinkage algorithms mitigate rank-deficiency by computing a convex combination that systematically blends the noisy sample covariance matrix with a highly structured target (such as the identity matrix).

However, because this blending is applied uniformly across the entire covariance structure, it inadvertently mixes the discriminative feature signal with isotropic noise across all available eigenvalues. This spectral flattening acts as a destructive regularizer in visual foundation spaces. By muting the high-variance principal axes that define class-specific directions, shrinkage methods fundamentally compress the structural contrast between distinct categories. In comparison, the Hybrid-Cov and Diag-Cov methods handle small-sample constraints through targeted structural parameterisation, ensuring that the prominent discriminative axes are preserved natively without receiving arbitrary isotropic dampening.

\subsection{Homoscedasticity and the Long-Horizon Joint Benchmark}
\label{sec:an:homoscedasticity}

The introduction of the long-horizon 30-task Joint Dataset Benchmark exposes a severe structural failure mode for traditional independent class density estimation, while validating the utility of homoscedastic approximations. As demonstrated in Table~\ref{tab:split_joint}, both Full-Cov and Hybrid-Cov experience a sharp performance drop, falling to an identical $59.35\%$ accuracy. 

This severe drop is a direct consequence of sample-size heterogeneity across the aggregated stream. Because individual components are pulled from CIFAR-100 ($n_c=400$), ImageNet-R ($n_c=40$), and Caltech-256 ($n_c=64$), the estimated covariance matrices for different categories possess entirely different empirical ranks. Consequently, the resulting log-determinant volume terms and Mahalanobis distances scale on completely disparate numerical bounds, rendering posterior comparisons between classes structurally invalid. 

The Shared-Full-Cov framework resolves this rank imbalance by enforcing a global homoscedastic assumption across the embedding space, achieving high accuracy on the Joint benchmark ($81.49\%$). By pooling the inner product sums into a singular, incrementally updated shared covariance matrix $\Sigma_{\text{shared}}$, the model ensures that every active class is evaluated against an identical statistical baseline. This regularizes the volume term across all categories by construction, demonstrating that when an incremental system is confronted with severely imbalanced or multi-domain streams, modeling a unified global feature noise structure is significantly more robust than estimating unconstrained, localized per-class variations.

\subsection{The Power of Shared Structural Covariances and MOG4-Shared}
\label{sec:an:shared_covariances}

The most remarkable result on the long-horizon, 30-task Joint Dataset stream is the overwhelming success of the MOG4-Shared configuration, which secures the highest overall accuracy at $84.49\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:split_joint}). This performance significantly outpaces not only the localized independent Gaussian models but also the shared single-component baselines, exposing critical nuances in how visual foundation model embeddings interact with second-order statistics under extreme distribution shifts.

As established, the architectural failure of independent second-order parameterisations under data imbalance stems from the unconstrained scaling of their volume constraints. The homoscedastic pooling layer of the Shared variants fixes this completely by enforcing a uniform baseline. Crucially, MOG4-Shared elevates this homoscedastic formulation by reintroducing representational flexibility exactly where it is most beneficial: the first-order location parameters. While a single shared Gaussian per class forces categories to be modeled as unimodal sub-spaces, MOG4-Shared allows the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) to dynamically assign up to four independent centroids per class, all scaled by the identical, robustly estimated global noise structure $\Sigma_{\text{shared}}$.

This specific combination yields two massive architectural advantages on the heterogeneous Joint stream:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Intra-Class Visual Heterogeneity:} For classes that exhibit high visual intra-class variance or stylistic multi-modality---a common challenge when cross-dataset images span low-resolution natural frames (CIFAR-100), artistic sketches (ImageNet-R), and clean product photography (Caltech-256)---the multiple centroids of the mixture model map separate visual sub-clusters elegantly without parameter interference.
	\item \textbf{Decoupled Parameter Budgets:} Because the components do not attempt to fit independent, unconstrained full covariance matrices, the free-parameter budget scales linearly with the number of mixtures rather than quadratically with the ambient feature dimension ($\mathcal{O}(K \cdot d)$ vs $\mathcal{O}(K \cdot d^2)$). The global covariance matrix absorbs the structural correlation signal from the deep multi-dataset pool, allowing individual mixture components to remain highly sample-efficient even when a new class arrives with a severely restricted sample budget.
\end{enumerate}

The dominance of MOG4-Shared over the single-component Shared-Full-Cov ($84.49\%$ vs. $81.49\%$) explicitly proves that once numerical stability is guaranteed via homoscedastic pooling, modeling multi-modal location statistics is the key to unlocking the maximum representational capacity of Visual Foundation Models in long-horizon incremental learning.

\subsection{Mixture Models and the Multi-Modality Question}
\label{sec:an:mog_extended}

The mixture variants MOG2-D/F/H and MOG4-D/F/H assess whether modelling multi-modal class-conditional distributions can yield additional gains under independent assumptions. The empirical pattern remains uneven. Mixture models slightly outperform a single Gaussian on Caltech-256---where intra-class variability is sufficient to justify two or four components---but offer at best a draw on CIFAR-100, where the class-conditional features are already well modelled by a single Gaussian. 

The most striking observation remains the parallel collapse of MOG4-F and MOG4-H on the Joint benchmark ($59\%$). Two compounding factors explain this collapse: the parameter budget of four full-covariance components per class in $d=768$ amounts to $1.18\times 10^{6}$ free parameters, an order of magnitude beyond the per-class sample budget inherited from Caltech-256; and the BIC selector embedded in the implementation occasionally favours a sub-optimal number of components when the underlying density is unimodal but rotated. The practical recommendation is therefore to deploy mixtures selectively, in settings where the class density is genuinely multi-modal or protected by shared global covariance metrics, and to avoid them otherwise.

\subsection{The Pathological Overconfidence of Full Covariance}
\label{sec:calibration:full_pathology}

The most prominent feature of Table~\ref{tab:calibration_metrics} is the complete saturation of the confidence profiles across every single benchmark under the Full covariance parameterisation. For CIFAR-100, ImageNet-R, Caltech-256, and the Joint stream alike, the Full covariance model yields a Confidence on Errors (CoE) of exactly $1.000$, a Total Confidence (TC) of $1.000$, a Probability Margin (PM) of $1.000$, and a predictive Entropy collapsing to $0.000$ (with a minor numerical artifact of $0.001$ on ImageNet-R). This behavior indicates a severe case of pathological overconfidence: whenever the model makes a prediction, it places $100\%$ of its softmax probability mass onto the winning class, completely blinding itself to alternative options.

This phenomenon is not an accidental property of the dataset distributions, but rather a direct, predictable outcome of the high-dimensional small-sample rank-deficiency challenge detailed in Section 3.4. When a full covariance matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma}_c \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is estimated from a restricted sample pool ($n_c < d = 768$), it is structurally rank-deficient. Bounding its near-zero eigenvalues using a fixed diagonal ridge regularization floor ($\epsilon = 10^{-6}\mathbf{I}$) allows for Cholesky inversion, but it introduces a severe artifact into the log-determinant volume calculation and the Mahalanobis quadratic form. 

Because the data natively resides within a lower-dimensional active subspace (as demonstrated by the rapid eigenvalue decay in Section 4.7), any test sample that departs slightly from this subspace along the unpopulated, regularized directions receives an astronomical Mahalanobis penalty. When mapped through the exponential operator of the Gaussian density function, the likelihood scores collapse exponentially fast toward zero. Consequently, a test query will typically be extremely close to only one specific class-conditional manifold in regularized space, while being vastly far away from the remaining $C-1$ class manifolds. When normalized via the softmax operator to generate posteriors, this extreme numerical scale difference completely zeros out the denominator for all runner-up categories. This causes the winning class to absorb the entire probability mass, driving the Probability Margin and Total Confidence to absolute saturation ($1.000$) while generating highly inflated Expected Calibration Errors (ECE), peaking at $37.44\%$ on ImageNet-R.

\subsection{The Honest Probabilistic Behavior of Diagonal Covariance}
\label{sec:calibration:diagonal_honesty}

In sharp contrast to the Full model's structural blindness, the Diagonal covariance model exhibits highly honest and well-calibrated probabilistic properties. By restricting $\mathbf{\Sigma}_c$ to its diagonal components, the framework drastically reduces parameter volatility, ensuring it remains highly well-conditioned even under severe data scarcity.

The Diagonal model's Total Confidence tracks its empirical accuracy tightly across all benchmarks. On CIFAR-100, an accuracy of $86.07\%$ is met with a Total Confidence of $0.836$, yielding an exceptional Expected Calibration Error of just $3.72\%$. This close alignment confirms that the Diag-Cov posterior distribution acts as a reliable gauge of objective correctness: the model is highly certain when it is correct, and systematically tempers its certainty when it is wrong. This is further validated by looking at the Confidence on Errors, which stays within a safe, moderate range ($0.467$ on CIFAR-100 and $0.656$ on ImageNet-R), ensuring that misclassifications are flagged with high internal uncertainty.

This well-conditioned behavior is also reflected in the steady gap between Total Confidence and the Probability Margin. Across the rows, TC and PM differ by a modest margin of $0.02$ to $0.06$ depending on the dataset ($0.836$ vs. $0.774$ on CIFAR-100; $0.883$ vs. $0.820$ on ImageNet-R). This soft gap mathematically proves that the Diagonal model does not uniformly dump all its probability mass onto a single guess; instead, it retains a meaningful runner-up probability ($p_{(2)} \approx 0.03$--$0.06$) for competitive classes. The gap naturally narrows on Caltech-256 ($TC=0.974$, $PM=0.952$) and the Joint stream ($TC=0.931$, $PM=0.880$) while predictive Entropy hits its lowest point ($0.054$). This behavior indicates that as the absolute visual separability of the underlying categories increases—which aligns with the exceptionally large inter-class Bhattacharyya distances tracked in Section 4.5—the model's posteriors naturally become more decisive without suffering from the regularized numerical distortions that plague the full-rank architecture.

Ultimately, these metrics establish a critical engineering trade-off for generative lifelong learning: while unconstrained Full covariance matrices can extract fine-grained correlational shape details to maximize raw accuracy in balanced domains, they do so at the cost of absolute calibration failure. For applications requiring meaningful uncertainty quantification, out-of-distribution discovery, or task-free deployment, the Diagonal model remains the only parameterisation where the posterior probability functions as an honest representation of actual performance.

\section{Incremental Class Inclusion}
\label{sec:incremental_update}

Figure~\ref{fig:avg_acc_evolution} reports the evolution of the average accuracy across ten sequential tasks for the Diag-Cov (\textbf{a}) and Full-Cov (\textbf{b}) estimators, on the three benchmarks (CIFAR-100, ImageNet-R, and Caltech-256). At each task index $t$, the average accuracy is computed over all classes seen so far (tasks $1$ through $t$), so the curves track how well the system continues to recognise the growing set of classes as new ones are incorporated.

On all three benchmarks and for both estimators, average accuracy declines monotonically as the number of tasks grows: from the mid-$90\,\%$ to high-$90\,\%$ range after the first task down to figures in the $60$--$90\,\%$ range after the tenth. Full-Cov on Caltech-256 shows the steepest single-step drop, falling from roughly $93\,\%$ to $74\,\%$ between tasks 5 and 6, after which it stabilises; Diag-Cov degrades more smoothly on every benchmark, and ImageNet-R exhibits the most gradual decline of the three datasets under either estimator.

This decline should not be read as forgetting in the conventional sense. Because every class-conditional Gaussian is estimated independently and frozen at the moment its class is inserted, no parameters belonging to previously learned classes are ever overwritten or updated when a new task arrives: each class retains, unchanged, the exact representation it was given when it was first learned. What grows with $t$ is not the error on old classes caused by interference from new ones, but the difficulty of the classification problem itself, since every additional task enlarges the set of competing prototypes among which the Bayesian decision rule must discriminate. This is the same mechanism quantified in Figure~\ref{fig:bhattacharyya} through the decay of the minimum pairwise Bhattacharyya distance: as more classes are added, some pairs of prototypes inevitably move closer together in feature space, increasing the chance of confusion, even though none of the individual class models has changed. In this sense the observed accuracy drop reflects a harder decision surface, not the loss of previously stored knowledge, and it is the structural counterpart of the low forgetting values already reported in Table~\ref{tab:combined_benchmarks}. This is the same mechanism that the pairwise Bhattacharyya distance quantifies directly; the next section develops that analysis in full.

\subsection{Interpreting the Separability and Geometric Curves}
\label{sec:an:interclass}

The sequential scaling trajectories of the pairwise Bhattacharyya distances (Figures~\ref{fig:bhattacharyya} and~\ref{fig:bhattacharyya_thresholds}) and mean Euclidean distances (Figures~\ref{fig:euclidean} and~\ref{fig:euclidean_thresholds}) reveal a highly consistent topological behavior across the foundation embedding manifolds. Rather than demonstrating an overall compression of the representation space as tasks accumulate, the global layout of the features remains remarkably stable, while performance boundaries are strictly dictated by a localized, heavy-tailed minority of highly overlapping class pairs.

A primary indication of this structural stability is that both macro-averaged distance metrics plateau rapidly across all benchmarks once approximately 30 categories are introduced. At full class-set capacity, the mean pairwise $D_B$ stabilizes at $220.61$ for Caltech-256, $89.43$ for CIFAR-100, and $84.81$ for ImageNet-R. This dataset hierarchy is flawlessly mirrored by the macro-averaged mean Euclidean distance, where Caltech-256 maintains a wide geometric separation of $\overline{d_E} = 0.978$, whereas CIFAR-100 and ImageNet-R remain more densely packed at $\overline{d_E} = 0.749$ and $\overline{d_E} = 0.727$, respectively. The pronounced separability advantage characteristic of Caltech-256 across both domains underscores its vast visual and semantic diversity, allowing per-class distributions to remain exceptionally distinct.

However, as the overall density of the label space increases, the minimum pairwise distance metrics decay monotonically, exposing the true driver behind accuracy drops in closed-form generative learning. At the final checkpoint, the minimum pairwise $D_B$ reaches severe proximity limits, dropping to $3.57$ on CIFAR-100, $11.54$ on ImageNet-R, and $20.96$ on Caltech-256. This localized crowding is corroborated by the Euclidean mean separation, which hits minimum boundaries of $0.144$, $0.205$, and $0.275$ for the respective datasets. 

Crucially, tracking the density thresholds reveals that this structural crowding is an isolated anomaly rather than a systemic space collapse. Over $99.8\,\%$ of all possible class combinations comfortably exceed a threshold of $D_B = 50$ on every evaluated benchmark. Similarly, class pairs with $d_E < 0.5$ occupy a minute fraction of the overall systems, accounting for only $0.91\,\%$ of pairs in CIFAR-100, $2.20\,\%$ in ImageNet-R, and an insignificant $0.04\,\%$ in Caltech-256. 

Consequently, the final class-incremental classifier is not forced to navigate a uniformly collapsed or muddy feature space. Instead, the systemic accuracy limits—stabilizing at $88.87\%$ for CIFAR-100, $77.99\%$ for ImageNet-R, and $94.36\%$ for Caltech-256 under the strongest Gaussian configurations—are directly bounded by the rare emergence of highly confusable prototype combinations. 

The two evaluated metrics act as vital, complementary diagnostics for these failure points. While $d_E$ isolates pure first-order geometric separation between prototype centroids, $D_B$ adds a penalization for covariance shape and volume dissimilarity. This explains why a pair such as \textit{boy}/\textit{girl} in CIFAR-100 is identified as the single most critical confusion sink in the system: it simultaneously combines a highly compressed geometric centroid distance ($d_E = 0.145$) with nearly identical, overlapping second-order covariance structures, driving the probabilistic separation down to its absolute minimum. The measured increase in conventional forgetting across tasks is therefore not an indicator of representation degradation—given that all historical sufficient statistics are explicitly frozen—but is instead a direct, quantifiable reflection of a progressively hardening decision surface where newly introduced categories happen to land within the probability mass of previously established class manifolds.

The shapes of these resulting histograms reflect fundamentally different data configurations within the embedding space:

On Split CIFAR-100, the recall profiles for both Diag-Cov and Full-Cov display a tightly concentrated, broadly unimodal geometry heavily skewed toward the highest accuracy bins. This symmetric, high-density concentration reflects the stationary and well-behaved nature of low-resolution natural features, where the vast majority of categories possess compact feature distributions that single-component Gaussians approximate with minimal residual variance. Transitioning to Split Caltech-256 reveals an even cleaner concentration peak; under Full-Cov estimation, the median shifts decisively toward $97.8\%$, compressing the bulk of the dataset into a near-perfect recognition cluster. This extreme concentration demonstrates that although high-resolution object photography introduces massive variance across coordinates, its semantic categories occupy highly isolated, linearly separable regions of the DINOv2 manifold, allowing a full precision matrix to successfully decouple the few remaining competitive boundaries.

Conversely, Split ImageNet-R presents a highly dispersed, flat distribution characterized by a long, heavy tail of low-accuracy classes extending down past the $40\%$ boundary. Unlike the tight concentration observed in CIFAR-100 and Caltech-256, the visual features of ImageNet-R are inherently un-concentrated. Because the imagery is intentionally out-of-distribution---spanning highly stylized art, cartoons, and hand-drawn sketches---the frozen foundation encoder maps these samples into broad, diffuse, and highly anisotropic representations. This lack of spatial concentration produces massive intra-class variance and complex directional skewness, which directly undercuts the modeling capacity of single independent Gaussians. 

This visual and structural dispersion directly connects with the aggregate statistics of the stream. The elongated failure tail of ImageNet-R is the physical expression of the localized crowding captured in the pairwise distance analysis, where a significantly higher proportion of class pairs drop below the critical separability threshold ($D_B < 50$) compared to the remaining datasets. While moving from Diag-Cov to Full-Cov on Caltech-256 succeeds because it maps the clean, rotated axes of a well-separated space, the gain on ImageNet-R is bounded precisely because no linear covariance transformation can artificially induce spatial concentration on a representation manifold that is fundamentally scattered by domain shifts.

Ultimately, these macro-level distributions hide the specific semantic failure modes that form the lower tails of our histograms. To discover why these specific categories fail to concentrate, we must shift from aggregate statistical bins to an explicit case study of individual class confusion patterns, examining the visual and geometric identities of the worst-performing archetypes in the system.

\subsection{Synthesis of Inter-Class Confusion and Structural Bounds}
\label{sec:an:confusion}

The pairwise relationships extracted from the final-state distributions (Tables~\ref{tab:worst_diag} and~\ref{tab:worst_full}) provide a localized explanation for the aggregate separability trends seen across the benchmarks. Rather than pointing to a systemic breakdown of the generative modeling framework, the residual classification error concentrates in a minute tail of highly confusable class pairs, providing a precise diagnostic of the representation's semantic boundaries.

This localized failure mode operates along three clear structural dimensions:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Semantic and Visual Near-Synonyms:} The most acute confusion is driven by pairs that share fundamental silhouette, texturing, or compositional profiles under DINOv2's representation. Archetypes such as \textit{boy}$\leftrightarrow$\textit{girl} ($D_B = 3.45$, $d_E = 0.145$), \textit{bow\_tie}$\leftrightarrow$\textit{scarf} ($D_B = 16.57$), and \textit{top-hat}$\leftrightarrow$\textit{cowboy-hat} ($D_B = 29.75$) represent co-located distributions where high-frequency discriminative features are largely absent from frozen, general-purpose embeddings.
	\item \textbf{Contextual and Manifold Sinks:} Proximity in the embedding space frequently arises from shared contextual background or composition rather than category membership. This is illustrated by the convergence of \textit{cloud}$\leftrightarrow$\textit{sea} ($D_B = 30.54$) via matching color palettes, and the emergence of broad categories like \textit{people} as prominent confusion sinks for unrelated objects (\textit{canoe}, \textit{drinking-straw}) that happen to be photographed with human subjects in frame.
	\item \textbf{The Mechanics of Apparent Forgetting:} This pairwise crowding provides a concrete definition for the modest rise in forgetting recorded throughout the stream. Because class-conditional models are estimated independently and frozen, the representations themselves suffer no backward degradation. Instead, apparent forgetting is driven strictly by the expansion of the label space, which systematically increases the mathematical probability of inserting a new category close to a previously learned one.
\end{itemize}

Crucially, this analysis exposes the limits of downstream parametric adaptation. Upgrading the modeling architecture from a Diagonal to a Full covariance matrix captures important correlational structure—recovering \textit{boy} from $50.0\,\%$ to $58.0\,\%$ recall—but cannot fully decouple distributions whose means are nearly coincident ($d_E \to 0$). Resolving this persistent overlap remains a task for representation alignment at the backbone level. To determine how severely the entire class-incremental pipeline depends on these native feature distributions, the next section evaluates the sensitivity of the Gaussian framework to the choice of the upstream feature extractor itself.

\subsection{Spectral Properties of the Foundation Embeddings}
\label{sec:an:spectrum}

The global PCA spectrum illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pca_eigenvalues} exposes three fundamental regularities that characterize the DINOv2 feature space:

First, all three benchmarks share a remarkably similar multi-stage variance allocation. The first principal component accounts for approximately 8\%--10\% of the total variance, followed by a sharp, exponential decay over the first $\sim\!50$ dimensions that collectively captures 36\%--40\% of the global variance. Beyond this point, the spectrum transitions into a long, flat, slowly-decaying tail across the remaining 700+ coordinates. 

Second, the absence of an abrupt truncation or truncation step in the spectrum implies that while the highly discriminative signal natively concentrates within a moderate-dimensional subspace, it is not entirely confined to a lower-dimensional manifold. Meaningful residual variability is spread across the entire ambient space, which structurally justifies our use of a full high-dimensional ($d=768$) generative model over a truncated PCA-reduced surrogate.

Third, regardless of the global spectrum, the effective rank of any localized, per-class empirical covariance matrix remains strictly upper-bounded by $n_c - 1$. Because a maximum of $n_c - 1$ directional axes can be populated by a sample size of $n_c$ centered observations, severe data scarcity ($n_c < d$) introduces a systemic rank-deficiency pathology. This mathematical constraint serves as the root cause of the numerical instabilities observed in unconstrained full-covariance estimation, which are evaluated under compression in the next subsection.

\section{Analysis of Gaussianity Assumptions}
\label{sec:gaussianity_results}

In this section, we examine whether the embedding space of a visual foundation model natively satisfies the Gaussian assumption. To this end, we conduct a rigorous statistical evaluation using the training split of each benchmark. Our analysis specifically focuses on DINOv2; because it is trained via self-supervised learning on a massive, diverse dataset without the semantic constraints of manual labels, it yields a highly continuous, transferable, and well-behaved representation space.

To demonstrate that these self-supervised features are consistently Gaussian, we organize our validation into two structural levels: (1) Univariate Gaussianity: we evaluate the marginal distributions of individual feature dimensions using moment-based diagnostics (skewness and excess kurtosis), the D'Agostino--Pearson formal omnibus normality test, and a per-coordinate application of the Henze--Zirkler (HZ) test. (2) Multivariate Gaussianity: we evaluate the joint distribution in its native high-dimensional space by formulating a specialized diagonal-covariance adaptation of the HZ test to circumvent sample-size rank deficiencies.

\subsection{Univariate Gaussianity: Moments and Formal Tests}
\label{sec:uv_gaussianity}

\textbf{Shape moments.}
We compute sample skewness and excess kurtosis for every $(class~c, dimension~j)$ pair:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathrm{Skew}}_{c,j} = \frac{\frac{1}{n_c}\sum_{i}(x_{i,j}-\mu_{c,j})^3}{\hat\sigma_{c,j}^{3}},\qquad
    \widehat{\mathrm{Kurt}}_{c,j} = \frac{\frac{1}{n_c}\sum_{i}(x_{i,j}-\mu_{c,j})^4}{\hat\sigma_{c,j}^{4}} - 3,
    \label{eq:moments_results}
\end{equation}
so that both quantities vanish exactly for a Gaussian.

\textbf{D'Agostino--Pearson omnibus test.}
The omnibus test combines the two shape statistics into:
\begin{equation}

\subsection{Multivariate Gaussianity: Diagonal Henze--Zirkler Test}
\label{sec:mv_gaussianity_results}

Per-dimension normality is necessary but not sufficient for the joint $d$-dimensional distribution to be Gaussian. We extend our analysis to evaluate the distribution in its native $d=768$ space. Crucially, computing a standard full-covariance Henze--Zirkler (HZ) test is mathematically intractable in this setting. Because the number of available samples per class is smaller than the feature dimensionality, the empirical full covariance matrix becomes singular and non-invertible, which prevents the calculation of standard Mahalanobis distances.

To circumvent this rank deficiency without resorting to destructive dimensionality reduction techniques (such as PCA), we formulate a diagonal-covariance adaptation of the HZ test. This variant computes distance metrics using explicit per-coordinate sample variances, effectively regularizing the space.

Under exact multivariate normality, $T_{\mathrm{HZ}} \to 1$ as $d \to \infty$; values close to $1$ indicate near-Gaussianity. Table~\ref{tab:gaussianity_hz} confirms that all three benchmarks concentrate tightly near the Gaussian reference $T_{\mathrm{HZ}} = 1$, validating the Gaussian assumption at the multivariate level under a diagonal model.

The clean multivariate convergence to $T_{\mathrm{HZ}} \approx 1.00$ across all three datasets confirms that the joint space scales properly under independent assumptions, providing a sound statistical justification for deploying independent-feature probabilistic classifiers in visual foundation spaces.

\subsection{Univariate Henze--Zirkler Test per Dimension}
\label{sec:uv_hz_gaussianity_results}

Complementing the multivariate diagonal test above with a finer-grained, per-dimension hypothesis test, we return to the univariate level using the Henze--Zirkler statistic. As a stricter, hypothesis-based normality criterion, we apply the Henze--Zirkler (HZ) test independently to every individual coordinate space ($d=1$) for each class-dimension pair $(c, j)$. Under the null hypothesis $H_0$ of exact univariate normality, the finite-sample expectation of the statistic is given by $\mathbb{E}_{H_0}[T_{\mathrm{HZ}}] = 1 - a^{-1/2} \left(1 + \frac{b^2}{a} + \frac{3b^4}{4a^2}\right)$, where $a = 1 + 2b^2$ and $b^2 = \frac{1}{2}(4/3)^{0.4}n_c^{-0.4}$ represents the smoothing parameter or bandwidth conditioned on the per-class sample size $n_c$.

\subsection{Empirical Validation of the Gaussian Assumption}
\label{sec:an:gauss}

The Gaussianity tests above confirm that the per-coordinate normality assumption is well supported across all three benchmarks: 96.1\,\% of CIFAR-100 coordinates, 85.9\,\% of ImageNet-R, and 74.7\,\% of Caltech-256 satisfy the informal symmetry cut-off $|\text{Skew}|<0.5$. The key distinction between the two covariance estimators follows directly from the $n_c < d$ regime. The \textbf{Diag-Cov} model estimates only $d$ parameters per class and is therefore always well-conditioned; its squared Mahalanobis distances are broadly distributed near the theoretical $\chi^2_{d=768}$ mode, with dispersion growing from CIFAR-100 to Caltech-256 in line with the heavy-tail ordering of Table~\ref{tab:gaussianity_stats}. The \textbf{Full-Cov} model, by contrast, requires estimating a $d\times d$ matrix from $n_c < d$ samples: the resulting covariance is rank-deficient, the eigenvalues outside the spanned subspace collapse to the regularisation floor, and the quadratic form becomes degenerate, producing a sharp spike at $\overline{n_c}-1$ (approximately 498, 168, and 103 on CIFAR-100, ImageNet-R, and Caltech-256 respectively) rather than spreading across the $\chi^2_{768}$ support. Crucially, this pathology is a property of the \emph{estimator}, not of the data---the per-coordinate distributions are demonstrably near-Gaussian. Preserving inter-feature correlations while avoiding rank deficiency remains an open challenge; the hybrid variant represents one practical way to trade off these two requirements.

Having validated the Gaussian assumption both marginally and jointly, we now turn to a synthesis of the findings collected across all the topics examined in this chapter.

\subsection{Computational Footprint and Practical Scalability}
\label{sec:an:compute}

Translating these algorithmic bounds into real-world, wall-clock performance highlights a fundamental operational advantage when contrasting this framework against prompt-based or replay-dependent alternatives. On Split CIFAR-100, integrating a newly encountered task into our generative framework requires approximately two minutes on a standard consumer-grade GPU. This time is spent exclusively on executing the deterministic, single-pass feature extraction of the raw images to calculate sufficient statistics. 

In comparison, prompt-tuning configurations documented in recent literature routinely require 10--30 minutes of training per task on equivalent hardware hardware due to the necessity of backpropagating gradient updates through extensive prompt pools over multiple training epochs. By completely bypassing backpropagation and eliminating the storage overhead of a physical rehearsal buffer, the proposed framework achieves a 5--15$\times$ training acceleration, positioning it as an ideal candidate for decentralized, low-resource edge deployment.

\subsection{Limitations and Architectural Open Questions}
\label{sec:an:limits}

While the empirical findings confirm the efficiency and structural calibration of this frozen generative approach, three specific constraints deserve explicit acknowledgment:

First, the current evaluation pipeline presumes that exact task boundaries are explicitly provided during the training phase to partition incoming batches. Relaxing this operational constraint to accommodate task-free continual learning constitutes a vital next step, testing whether the decoupled Gaussian updates remain robust when categorical boundaries are completely blurred or interleaved.

Second, our experimental validation has focused exclusively on visual classification streams. Although the underlying generative mechanics remain fundamentally agnostic to the native input modality, executing a parallel empirical validation on transformer-encoded text or speech embeddings is required to verify if the clean Gaussian behavior observed here generalizes across multi-modal representation spaces.

Third, a highly compelling extension lies in leveraging the well-behaved calibration profiles of our independent configurations for open-world discovery. The severe overconfidence of the Full covariance model renders its predictive metrics useless outside its closed label space. However, the highly calibrated Predictive Entropy and Probability Margin of the Diagonal model can be directly utilized as natural out-of-distribution anomaly scores. Investigating this capability will determine if the robust generative framework can successfully flag novel, unlearned semantic categories while preserving its historical performance boundaries.

\section{Methodological Synthesis and Engineering Paradigm}
\label{sec:an:summary}

The empirical evaluations compiled throughout this chapter provide a comprehensive validation of the structural behavior, computational footprint, and parametric limitations of closed-form generative classifiers operating on frozen foundation embeddings. Synthesizing these multi-dimensional results reveals that the class-incremental learning problem can be effectively shifted from a complex struggle against catastrophic forgetting to a structured task of density estimation within a stationary representation space. The native feature manifold generated by DINOv2 shows exceptional compliance with marginal normality assumptions, passing coordinate-level Gaussianity thresholds across $96\,\%$ of dimensions in Split CIFAR-100, $86\,\%$ in Split ImageNet-R, and $75\,\%$ in Split Caltech-256. Because the global representation remains stable as tasks accumulate, the resulting quadratic and linear decision boundaries yield performance competitive with optimization-heavy, prompt-based architectures while remaining completely deterministic, closed-form, and gradient-free.

The ultimate classification ceiling of this framework is explicitly dictated by the spatial resolution of the upstream feature extractor rather than the capacity of the downstream classifier, as evidenced by the large performance gaps observed across differing visual backbones. This downstream classification error is not distributed uniformly; instead, it is tightly localized within a heavy-tailed minority representing fewer than $0.2\,\%$ of total class combinations where the pairwise Bhattacharyya distance drops below a critical threshold ($D_B < 20$). These failure modes isolate semantically near-synonymous or visually continuous categories---such as \textit{boy}/\textit{girl} at low resolutions or \textit{top-hat}/\textit{cowboy-hat} and \textit{bow\_tie}/\textit{scarf} under domain shifts. This structural overlap establishes an unresolvable decision boundary that second-order statistics alone cannot fully decouple, meaning that apparent forgetting in frozen streams is actually a direct reflection of newly introduced categories landing within the probability mass of previously established class manifolds.

Managing these local overlap boundaries requires a deployment strategy tailored directly to the sample-size budget and categorical homogeneity of the incoming stream. When the per-class sample allocation comfortably outpaces the feature dimensionality, unconstrained full covariance modeling computed via the regularized Cholesky-based log-determinant framework provides the most robust configuration, successfully mapping off-diagonal feature correlations. In data-scarce yet homogeneous regimes, this Cholesky-parameterized full or hybrid model remains highly effective by utilizing precise volumetric tracking to separate class boundaries. However, under the severe sample-size heterogeneity of mixed-source datasets, unconstrained covariance parameters collapse due to imbalanced regularizing terms. In these heterogeneous deployment scenarios, shared covariance estimators represent the only viable fallback, as pooling second-order statistics across the active label space effectively decouples the numerical stability of the covariance inversion from the specific sample depth of any single incoming category.

Ultimately, these structural insights confirm the central thesis-level claim that lifelong learning can be addressed as a problem of static statistical modeling rather than dynamic parameter optimization. By utilizing frozen sufficient statistics, the framework eliminates representation drift by design. The practical challenge of incremental learning is thus distilled into selecting the specific covariance parameterization and evidence-fusion strategy that best align with the sample density and semantic granularity of the deployment scenario. This structural framework converts a historically unconstrained learning problem into a highly predictable engineering workflow, laying a rigorous mathematical foundation for the comprehensive system-level conclusions and future representation alignment paradigms detailed in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}.
