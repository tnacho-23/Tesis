\chapter{Statistical and Probabilistic Foundations of the Feature Space}
\label{ch:gaussian_space}

The framework proposed in this thesis rests on a single working hypothesis: that the class-conditional feature distributions produced by a frozen visual foundation model are well approximated by multivariate Gaussians, and that the resulting decision surfaces can be described in terms of the geometry of the embedding space they live in. This chapter puts together, in one self-contained treatment, the mathematical and statistical tools used throughout this thesis to build and check that hypothesis. We start with the probabilistic modelling framework itself---Bayesian inference, the Maximum A Posteriori decision rule, and its Mixture-of-Gaussians extension. We then turn directly to the Gaussianity assumption these models depend on, presenting the moment-based diagnostics and the formal hypothesis tests, univariate first and then multivariate, including the Henze--Zirkler statistic and its diagonal adaptation. With that assumption in place, we introduce the inter-class separability metrics---the probabilistic Bhattacharyya distance and the geometric Euclidean distance---used to quantify how classes crowd the embedding space as new tasks arrive. We then explain why estimating a covariance matrix in this setting is difficult in the first place, a difficulty commonly known as the curse of dimensionality, and how it can be addressed through regularization; this naturally leads into the spectral tools---Principal Component Analysis and the associated eigenvalue-decay diagnostics---used to describe the intrinsic dimensionality of the feature space and, in turn, to understand why that regularization is needed.

\section{Foundations of Probabilistic Modeling}
\label{sec:framework:probabilistic}

Representing each class as a Gaussian density only makes sense if there is a solid decision rule behind it that says how to actually use that density to classify a new observation. This section builds that rule from the ground up: starting from Bayes' theorem, we arrive at the Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) criterion, which tells us how to pick the most likely class given the observed features, and then extend it to mixtures of Gaussians for classes whose features are better described by more than one cluster. 
\subsection{Bayes Classification}

At its core, Bayes classification is rooted in \textbf{Bayes' Theorem}, which provides a mathematical framework for updating the probability of a hypothesis based on observed evidence. For a classification task, we seek the posterior probability $p(y=c \mid \vec{x})$—the probability that an observation belongs to class $c$ given its observed feature vector $\vec{x}$.

Bayes' Theorem is defined as:
\begin{equation}
	p(y=c \mid \vec{x}) = \frac{p(\vec{x} \mid y=c) \, p(y=c)}{p(\vec{x})},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
	\item $p(y=c \mid \vec{x})$ is the \textbf{posterior} probability.
	\item $p(\vec{x} \mid y=c)$ is the \textbf{likelihood} of the data given the class.
	\item $p(y=c)$ is the \textbf{prior} probability of the class.
	\item $p(\vec{x})$ is the \textbf{evidence} or marginal likelihood, acting as a normalization constant.
\end{itemize}

The objective of a Bayes classifier is to minimize the Bayes risk; in the simplest case, this corresponds to assigning the observation to the class that maximizes the posterior probability.

\subsection{Likelihood-Based Classification}

Likelihood inference provides a coherent framework for updating the probability of a hypothesis as more evidence or information becomes available. In contrast to frequentist approaches that treat parameters as fixed but unknown constants, the Bayesian paradigm treats parameters as random variables characterized by probability distributions.

The connection between Bayes' Theorem and likelihood-based classification lies in the \textbf{Maximum A Posteriori (MAP)} decision rule. Since the evidence $p(\vec{x})$ is constant across all classes for a given observation, the classification decision depends solely on the product of the likelihood and the prior:
\begin{equation}
	p(y=c \mid \vec{x}) \propto p(\vec{x} \mid y=c) \, p(y=c).
\end{equation}

A generative modeling framework can be adopted and class-conditional Gaussian feature distributions can be assumed:
\begin{equation}
	p(\vec{x} \mid y=c)
	=
	\mathcal{N}(\vec{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_c, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c),
\end{equation}
where $\vec{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents the feature vector, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c$ is the class mean vector, and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c \succ 0$ is the covariance matrix.

Classification can be performed via MAP inference by maximizing the log-posterior:
\begin{equation}
	\hat{y}
	=
	\arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{Y}}
	\left[
	\log p(\vec{x} \mid y=c) + \log \pi_c
	\right],
\end{equation}
where $\pi_c = p(y=c)$ denotes the class prior.

Substituting the multivariate Gaussian density into the MAP rule, the decision rule becomes equivalent to:
\begin{equation}
	\hat{y}
	=
	\arg\min_{c}
	\left[
	(\vec{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^T
	\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c^{-1}
	(\vec{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_c)
	+
	\log |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c|
	-
	2\log \pi_c
	\right].
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Mixture of Gaussians (MoG)}

A Mixture of Gaussians models a complex probability density as a convex combination of $K$ distinct Gaussian components. This approach is fundamental for clustering and density estimation in multi-modal distributions. Therefore, to capture multimodal feature distributions, the class-conditional model can be extended to a mixture:

\begin{equation}
	p(\vec{x} \mid y=c)
	=
	\sum_{k=1}^{K_c}
	\alpha_{c,k}
	\mathcal{N}(\vec{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c,k}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{c,k}),
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{c,k} \ge 0$ and $\sum_k \alpha_{c,k} = 1$.

The MAP decision rule becomes
\begin{equation}
	\hat{y}
	=
	\arg\max_c
	\left[
	\log
	\left(
	\sum_{k=1}^{K_c}
	\alpha_{c,k}
	\mathcal{N}(\vec{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c,k}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{c,k})
	\right)
	+
	\log \pi_c
	\right].
\end{equation}

This whole classification framework---from the plain Bayes rule to its Gaussian and mixture-of-Gaussians instantiations---is only as good as the assumption it is built on: that class-conditional features are actually well described by a Gaussian in the first place. The remainder of this chapter puts that assumption to work. Section~\ref{sec:method:gaussianity_main} tests it directly through moment-based diagnostics and formal hypothesis tests; Section~\ref{sec:method:separability} studies how the resulting Gaussians relate to one another as more classes are added; Section~\ref{sec:gsc:covariance} addresses the practical difficulty of estimating a covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c$ in a high-dimensional, small-sample regime; and Section~\ref{sec:gsc:spectral} closes the chapter by examining the geometric structure of the space these covariances live in.

\section{Gaussianity Assumptions and Analysis}
\label{sec:method:gaussianity_main}

Gaussianity, in simple terms, is just how well a set of data points can be described by a normal distribution: a bell-shaped curve that, in one dimension, is fully defined by a mean and a variance, or, in many dimensions at once, by a mean vector and a covariance matrix. The classifier proposed in this thesis works by fitting one such Gaussian to each class and then, for a new observation, comparing how likely that observation is under each of these class-specific Gaussians. This only makes sense if the features actually produced for each class really do look Gaussian. If they do not, the mean and covariance we estimate stop being a good description of the data, and the classifier can end up making confident, well-defined decisions that are simply wrong, even though the underlying math is correct. Checking Gaussianity before trusting the classifier is therefore not a formality: it tells us whether modelling each class as a single Gaussian is actually a reasonable choice for this particular feature space, rather than just a convenient one.

We check this assumption at two levels. We first look at \emph{per-dimension} (univariate) Gaussianity: whether each individual coordinate of the feature vector, taken on its own, looks Gaussian. We then look at \emph{per-class} (multivariate) Gaussianity: whether the full, high-dimensional feature vector of each class follows a joint Gaussian distribution.

\subsection{Per-Dimension Gaussianity}
\label{sec:method:gaussianity:moments}
\phantomsection\label{sec:method:gaussianity:normaltest}
\phantomsection\label{sec:method:gaussianity:univariate_concept}

We start at the simplest level: looking at each coordinate of the feature vector on its own, one at a time, and asking how close its distribution is to a normal curve. To verify Gaussianity at this per-dimension level, we combine two descriptive shape diagnostics with a formal statistical test, applying all of them to every (class~$c$, dimension~$j$) pair.

\subsubsection{Shape moments.}
Skewness and kurtosis are both examples of what statisticians call \emph{moments}: numerical summaries computed from the data that describe the shape of its distribution, in the same way that the mean and variance are moments that describe its location and spread. Concretely, kurtosis and skewness describe the shape of a distribution:

The \textbf{kurtosis} measures the ``tailedness'' and peakedness of a probability distribution relative to a normal distribution \cite{joyce2011kurtosis}. As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:kurtosis}, a perfectly Gaussian distribution has an excess kurtosis of zero (mesokurtic). Distributions with high kurtosis (leptokurtic, $Kurtosis > 0$) exhibit sharper peaks and heavier tails, indicating a higher concentration of data around the mean and a greater likelihood of extreme outliers. Conversely, low kurtosis (platykurtic, $Kurtosis < 0$) indicates a flatter peak and thinner tails.

\begin{figure}[htb]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.37\textwidth]{imagenes/marco/kurtosis.png}
	\caption{Visual representation of kurtosis levels: leptokurtic ($>0$), mesokurtic ($=0$) and platykurtic ($<0$) distributions \cite{joyce2011kurtosis}.}
	\label{fig:kurtosis}
\end{figure}

 The \textbf{skewness} quantifies the asymmetry of a distribution around its mean. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:skewness}, a symmetric distribution (center) is a fundamental assumption of the Gaussian model, implying that the mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c$, median, and mode coincide, providing an adequate representation of the class prototype within the embedding space. A positive skew (right) indicates a distribution with a long tail extending toward higher values, while a negative skew (left) indicates a long tail extending toward lower values. Significant skewness would suggest that the class-conditional mean is no longer a central representation of the feature distribution.

\begin{figure}[htb]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{imagenes/marco/skeweness.jpg}
	\caption{Comparison of negatively-skewed, symmetric and positively-skewed distributions \cite{pearson1895skewness}.}
	\label{fig:skewness}
\end{figure}

Following the normality diagnostics of Pearson~\cite{pearson1895skewness} and D'Agostino and Pearson~\cite{dagostino1971omnibus,dagostino1973tests}, we compute the sample skewness and excess kurtosis for each class~$c$ along each of the $d$ coordinates:
\begin{equation}
	\widehat{\mathrm{Skew}}_{c,j} \;=\; \frac{\frac{1}{n_c}\sum_{i}(x_{i,j}-\mu_{c,j})^3}{\hat\sigma_{c,j}^{3}},\qquad
	\widehat{\mathrm{Kurt}}_{c,j} \;=\; \frac{\frac{1}{n_c}\sum_{i}(x_{i,j}-\mu_{c,j})^4}{\hat\sigma_{c,j}^{4}} - 3,
	\label{eq:moments}
\end{equation}
where both quantities are computed exactly, unlike the qualitative reading of Figures~\ref{fig:kurtosis} and \ref{fig:skewness} above, and vanish exactly for a theoretical Gaussian distribution~\cite{pearson1895skewness,joyce2011kurtosis}.

Empirical evidence suggests that for many real-world datasets, both kurtosis and skewness typically fall within the range $[-1,1]$, with values in that range indicating distributions close to symmetric and free of excessively heavy tails \cite{hair2019multivariate}.

\subsubsection{D'Agostino--Pearson test}

This range is useful, but it is not, by itself, a formal test: looking at a single kurtosis or skewness value cannot tell us how confident we should be that a departure from zero is a real deviation from normality rather than plain sampling noise. The D'Agostino--Pearson test closes this gap by turning skewness and kurtosis into a single, joint hypothesis test~\cite{dagostino1971omnibus,dagostino1973tests}, applied to every (class, dimension) pair. Concretely, it converts each moment into a standardized score ($Z_1$ for skewness, $Z_2$ for kurtosis) and adds their squares into one combined statistic:
\begin{equation}
	K^2 \;=\; Z_1\!\left(\widehat{\mathrm{Skew}}\right)^2 + Z_2\!\left(\widehat{\mathrm{Kurt}}\right)^2 \;\sim\; \chi^2_2 \quad \text{under } H_0,
	\label{eq:normaltest}
\end{equation}
where $H_0$ is the hypothesis that the coordinate's data is normally distributed. Under $H_0$, $K^2$ follows a known chi-squared distribution with two degrees of freedom, which lets us compute an exact probability (a $p$-value) for how extreme the observed $K^2$ is; we reject $H_0$, and conclude the coordinate is not Gaussian, whenever this probability falls below the significance level $\alpha=0.05$. 

\subsubsection{Relaxed criterion.}
The D'Agostino--Pearson test becomes more sensitive as the sample size $n_c$ grows: with enough samples, even small, practically harmless departures from normality become statistically significant, causing the test to reject $H_0$ more often than is practically useful. To compensate for this, we add a second, size-independent check based directly on the reference range introduced earlier in this section: a dimension is classified as near-Gaussian if
\begin{equation}
	|\mathrm{Skew}| < 1 \quad \text{and} \quad |\mathrm{Kurt}| < 1
\end{equation}
hold simultaneously. 

\subsubsection{Henze--Zirkler test per dimension.}
\label{sec:method:gaussianity:hz_univariate}
The tests above only look at the shape of each coordinate's distribution, through its skewness and kurtosis. As a stricter check, we also apply the Henze--Zirkler (HZ) test~\cite{henze1990class} to every coordinate on its own: instead of summarising the data with a couple of shape numbers, it directly compares how the data is distributed against how a true Gaussian would be. The HZ statistic is defined for a feature vector of any dimension $d$; here we use its simplest form, one coordinate at a time ($d=1$), applied independently to every (class~$c$, dimension~$j$) pair. The same statistic, now with $d$ equal to the full embedding dimension, is used again in Section~\ref{sec:method:gaussianity:multivariate}.

Given $n_c$ centred samples $z_i = x_i - \hat{\mu}_{c,j}$ and a bandwidth $b>0$, the statistic is built from three simple terms, using the squared, variance-normalised distance $d_M^2(\cdot,\cdot)$ between two values:

\begin{equation}
	A = \frac{1}{n_c^2} \sum_{j=1}^{n_c} \sum_{k=1}^{n_c} e^{-\frac{b^2}{2}d_M^2(z_j, z_k)},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	B = \frac{2}{n_c}(1+b^2)^{-1/2} \sum_{j=1}^{n_c} e^{-\frac{b^2}{2(1+b^2)}d_M^2(z_j, 0)},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	C = (1+2b^2)^{-1/2}.
\end{equation}

In plain terms, $A$ checks how similar the samples are to one another, $B$ checks how quickly the data thins out around the mean, and $C$ is simply the value $B$ would take for a perfect Gaussian. Put together, the final statistic is:
\begin{equation}
	T_{n,b} = n_c \cdot \left[ A - B + C \right].
\end{equation}
$T_{n,b}$ stays close to a fixed reference value when the data really is Gaussian, and moves away from it when the data is more tightly clustered or more heavy-tailed than a Gaussian would be.

\paragraph{Bandwidth.}
For $d=1$, the bandwidth reduces to a simple function of the sample size alone:
\begin{equation}
	b^2 \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^{2/5} n_c^{-2/5}.
	\label{eq:hz_bw_univariate}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Fast computation.}
Computing $A$ directly means comparing all $n_c^2$ pairs of samples, which becomes costly once this has to be repeated for every coordinate. We avoid this by expanding the exponential in $A$ as a short Taylor series:
\begin{equation}
	A_m \;=\; \frac{1}{n_c^2}\sum_{j,k} e^{-\frac{b^2}{2}(z_{j,m}-z_{k,m})^2}
	\;=\; \frac{1}{n_c^2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}
	\frac{b^{2\ell}}{\ell!}\,S_\ell^2\;+\;\mathcal{O}\!\left((b^2)^{L+1}\right),
	\label{eq:hz_taylor}
\end{equation}
where $S_\ell = \sum_j v_{j,m}\,z_{j,m}^\ell$ and $v_{j,m} = \exp(-b^2 z_{j,m}^2 / 2)$. Using $L=8$ terms keeps the approximation error below $10^{-6}$, while letting us compute all $d$ coordinates at once instead of one at a time.

\paragraph{What to expect under $H_0$.}
If a coordinate is truly Gaussian ($H_0$), $T_{n,b}$ should fluctuate around a known reference value rather than around zero. For a general dimension $d$, this reference is approximately
\begin{equation}
	\mathbb{E}_{H_0}[T_{\mathrm{HZ}}] \;=\; 1 - (1 + 2b^2)^{-d/2},
	\label{eq:hz_expected_general}
\end{equation}
a formula we reuse, with $d$ equal to the full embedding dimension, in Section~\ref{sec:method:gaussianity:multivariate}. For the univariate case treated here ($d=1$), this approximation is not accurate enough, so we use the exact expectation instead:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbb{E}_{H_0}[T_{\mathrm{HZ}}] \;=\; 1 - a^{-1/2}\left(1 + \frac{b^2}{a} + \frac{3b^4}{4a^2}\right), \qquad a = 1+2b^2,
	\label{eq:hz_expected_univariate}
\end{equation}
with $b^2$ as given above. For $n_c \in [60,\,400]$, this gives a small bandwidth ($b^2 \approx 0.04$) and a near-zero baseline ($\mathbb{E}_{H_0}[T_{\mathrm{HZ}}] \approx 0.04$). In other words, a genuinely Gaussian coordinate should give a $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}$ value fluctuating tightly around this near-zero baseline.

\subsection{Per-Class Gaussianity}
\label{sec:method:gaussianity:multivariate}

We now move from testing one coordinate at a time to testing each class's full feature vector at once, across all $d$ dimensions together. This uses the same HZ statistic $T_{n,b}$ from Section~\ref{sec:method:gaussianity:hz_univariate}, just with $d$ set to the full embedding dimension instead of $d=1$. This joint test is needed because per-coordinate normality is not enough on its own: a set of coordinates can each look Gaussian by itself while still not being jointly Gaussian, if the way they relate to each other does not match what a multivariate Gaussian would look like.

Applying the test this way runs into a problem, though. Both $A$ and $B$ need the Mahalanobis distance, and computing it requires inverting the class covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c$. Whenever a class has fewer samples than feature dimensions ($n_c < d$), which is the case throughout this thesis, the estimated covariance matrix cannot be inverted, so the standard version of the test simply cannot be computed.

To work around this without dropping any dimensions, we use a simplified, diagonal version of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c$: instead of inverting the full matrix, we only invert its $d$ individual per-coordinate variances, treating the coordinates as unrelated for this purpose. A small constant ($\varepsilon = 10^{-10}$) is added to these variances so that coordinates with almost no spread do not cause a division by a near-zero number. This simplified version can always be computed, no matter how small $n_c$ is, which is what makes the test usable in this setting.

One thing changes when we switch to this high-dimensional, diagonal version of the test: the reference value $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}$ should be close to under $H_0$. The general formula is the same one given earlier,
\begin{equation}
	\mathbb{E}_{H_0}[T_{\mathrm{HZ}}] \;=\; 1 - (1+2b^2)^{-d/2},
\end{equation}
but here $d$ is large, so the term $(1+2b^2)^{-d/2}$ becomes essentially zero even for a modest bandwidth $b$, and the whole expression becomes close to $1$ instead of the near-$0$ value used in the univariate case. In practice, this means that a class whose features really are Gaussian should give a $T_{\mathrm{HZ}}$ value close to $1$, not close to $0$.

It is important to clarify explicitly that, because our modified HZ test enforces a diagonal covariance matrix to handle this small-sample regime, it does not mathematically guarantee full, arbitrary multivariate Gaussianity, where the covariances between every pair of dimensions would be fully unconstrained. However, given how severe the data limitation is for each class relative to the embedding dimension ($n_c < d$), this evaluation serves as a highly robust and reliable approximation for that stronger, unconstrained notion of Gaussianity.

\section{Inter-Class Structural Separability Analysis}
\label{sec:method:separability}

To understand how the Bayesian decision surface scales and characterize the geometric crowding of the embedding space as new tasks are incorporated, we evaluate inter-class relationships using two complementary metrics: the probabilistic Bhattacharyya distance and the geometric Euclidean distance.

\subsection{Pairwise Bhattacharyya Distance}
\label{sec:method:separability:bhattacharyya}

The \emph{inter-class} pairwise Bhattacharyya distance measures how distinguishable any two class-conditional Gaussians are from one another.
The Bhattacharyya distance is symmetric and provides a direct connection to the Bayes error rate via the Bhattacharyya coefficient $\rho_{ij} = e^{-D_B(i,j)}$.
Under the diagonal-Gaussian parameterisation, the Bhattacharyya distance between classes $i$ and $j$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
	D_B(i,j)
	= \frac{1}{8}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j)^{\top}
	\bar{\Sigma}^{-1}
	(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j)
	+ \frac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(
	\frac{\det\bar{\Sigma}}{\sqrt{\det\Sigma_i\,\det\Sigma_j}}
	\right),
	\label{eq:interclass_bhattacharyya}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\Sigma} = (\Sigma_i + \Sigma_j)/2$.
Because both $\Sigma_i$ and $\bar{\Sigma}$ are diagonal, all matrix operations reduce to element-wise arithmetic: determinants become products of the diagonal entries (or sums of logs), and the matrix inverse reduces to element-wise reciprocals.
A value of $D_B=0$ indicates complete distributional overlap; larger values correspond to greater separability and a lower Bhattacharyya coefficient $\rho = e^{-D_B}$.

\subsection{Pairwise Mean Euclidean Distance}
\label{sec:method:separability:euclidean}

While the Bhattacharyya distance jointly accounts for shifts in both first-order (means) and second-order (covariances) statistics, it can mask whether class texturing or raw prototype drift is the dominant factor in class confusion. Therefore, we isolate first-order geometric separation by calculating the plain pairwise Euclidean distance between the estimated class mean vectors $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$ and $\boldsymbol{\mu}_j$:
\begin{equation}
	d_E(i,j) = \|\boldsymbol{\mu}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d} (\mu_{i,k} - \mu_{j,k})^2},
	\label{eq:interclass_euclidean}
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the nominal dimensionality of the visual foundation embedding space.

\subsection{Stream Statistics and Trajectory Tracking}
\label{sec:method:separability:tracking}

As the total number of observed classes $k$ scales throughout the continual learning stream, we track three distinct statistics for both $D_B$ and $d_E$ across the expanding class-pair pool:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item[(a)] The \textbf{macro-averaged pairwise distance} ($\overline{D_B}$ and $\overline{d_E}$) flanked by a $[\mathrm{P}_{10},\mathrm{P}_{90}]$ percentile band to observe global drift.
	\item[(b)] The \textbf{minimum pairwise distance} ($\min D_B$ and $\min d_E$), which explicitly identifies the hardest, most confusable class pair in the system.
	\item[(c)] The \textbf{cumulative count of overlapping pairs} falling below critical proximity thresholds (e.g., $D_B \in \{10, 50, 100\}$ and $d_E \in \{0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}$) to capture the heavy-tailed growth of localized confusion sinks.
\end{enumerate}
Together, these metrics bridge empirical classification failure modes with the underlying statistical architecture of the learned representations.

\section{Covariance Estimation in High-Dimensional Spaces}
\label{sec:gsc:covariance}

A fundamental challenge in multivariate statistical analysis and pattern recognition is the estimation of the covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ from a finite set of $n$ observations. In classical low-dimensional regimes, where the sample size $n$ is much larger than the feature dimension $d$ ($n \gg d$), the empirical Sample Covariance Matrix (SCM) acts as a reliable, unbiased estimator. However, modern engineering applications—such as analyzing high-dimensional deep feature representations—frequently operate in the ``high-dimension, small-sample-size'' (HDSS) regime, where $d$ is of the order of hundreds or thousands, while $n$ remains strictly limited.

Under these conditions, classical multivariate analysis suffers severely from the \textit{curse of dimensionality}, leading to critical theoretical and numerical breakdowns in statistical modeling.

\subsection{The Curse of Dimensionality and Covariance Degeneracy}

When the dimensionality $d$ scales while the sample size $n$ remains fixed or grows at a slower rate, several geometric and algebraic anomalies emerge:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Singularity and Rank Deficiency:} Structurally, the SCM is calculated as the sum of $n$ outer products of centered data vectors. Consequently, the maximum rank of the SCM is bounded by $\min(n-1, d)$. In the typical HDSS scenario where $n < d$, the SCM has at least $d - n + 1$ zero eigenvalues. The resulting matrix is singular (rank-deficient) and cannot be inverted.

	\item \textbf{Loss of Negative Log-Likelihood and Distance Metrics:} Many fundamental statistical classifiers and density estimators—such as Gaussian Discriminant Analysis (GDA) or Mahalanobis distance formulations—depend heavily on the precision matrix (the inverse covariance, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}$) and the log-determinant $\ln|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|$. When the estimated covariance is singular, the log-determinant diverges to $-\infty$, and the precision matrix is undefined, rendering density estimation mathematically impossible.

	\item \textbf{Eigenvalue Distortion (Spreading Phenomenon):} Even in moderately high dimensions where $n \ge d$ and the SCM is technically invertible, the sample eigenvalues do not converge to their true counterparts. As proved in random matrix theory (e.g., the Marčenko-Pastur law \cite{MarPas67}), high-dimensional sample eigenvalues exhibit severe bias: the largest eigenvalues are systematically overestimated, while the smallest eigenvalues are underestimated. This artificial dispersion distorts the perceived geometric structure of the data.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Theoretical Foundations of Regularization}

The problems described above all trace back to the same cause: using the plain SCM in a regime where it cannot be trusted. The standard fix is \textit{regularization}: we stop trying to estimate the covariance matrix as precisely as possible, and instead accept a small, controlled amount of bias in exchange for a much more stable estimate.

In practice, regularization is implemented through two main strategies:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Spectral regularization and conditioning}~\cite{Friedman1989}: this strategy works directly on the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix. Since the problem is that some eigenvalues are zero or too close to zero, the fix is to push them up a bit, either by setting a minimum allowed eigenvalue or, more simply, by adding a small constant to the diagonal of the matrix (often called diagonal loading). This keeps every eigenvalue safely above zero, so the matrix stays invertible and its inverse does not blow up.

	\item \textbf{Structured shrinkage}~\cite{Ledoit2004,Chen2010}: this strategy takes a different route. Instead of correcting individual eigenvalues, it blends the unstable sample covariance matrix with a much simpler, well-behaved ``target'' matrix (for example, the identity matrix, scaled appropriately, or a diagonal matrix). The final estimate is a weighted average of the two, and by shrinking it toward this stable target, the distorted, spread-out eigenvalues of the sample covariance are pulled back closer to their true values, reducing the mean squared error of the estimate.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Spectral Geometry of the Feature Space}
\label{sec:gsc:spectral}

We now look at how variance is spread across the $d$ coordinates of the embedding---that is, whether the data varies a lot along every one of its $d$ dimensions, or whether most of that variation is actually concentrated in just a few of them, with the rest being close to constant. This matters because it directly affects both how trustworthy our Gaussian modelling is and how well the covariance estimators of the previous section can be expected to behave. We use Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to look at this spread, then use what it shows us, called \emph{spectral decay}, to ask a simple question: is the feature space really as high-dimensional as it looks, or can it be described well with far fewer dimensions?

\subsection{Principal Component Analysis}
\label{sec:marco:pca}

PCA is a standard and widely used technique~\cite{jolliffe2002principal}: given a centred data matrix $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$, PCA diagonalises its empirical covariance matrix, $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{P}^{T}$, where the columns of $\mathbf{P}$ are orthonormal directions in the feature space (the \emph{principal components}), and the diagonal of $\boldsymbol{\Lambda}$ holds their associated eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0$. Each eigenvalue simply tells us how much variance in the data lies along its corresponding direction, so together they describe exactly how the total variance of the embedding is distributed across the $d$ dimensions.

\subsection{Spectral Decay and Effective Dimensionality}
\label{sec:gsc:spectral_decay}

We call the sequence of eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_d$, sorted from largest to smallest, the \emph{spectrum} of the feature space, and how quickly it shrinks is its \emph{decay}. A fast decay means that a handful of directions already explain most of the variance, and the remaining directions add very little; a slow, flat decay means variance is spread out roughly evenly across most of the $d$ dimensions. Concretely, the fraction of total variance explained by the leading $k$ components is
\begin{equation}
	R(k) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_i}.
	\label{eq:pca_variance_ratio}
\end{equation}

This matters for two practical reasons. First, if $R(k)$ is already close to $1$ for some $k \ll d$, the data effectively lives in a $k$-dimensional subspace, even though it is represented using $d$ numbers; we call this its \emph{effective dimensionality}, and it is often much smaller than $d$. Second, this tells us whether the feature space could be safely compressed: if most of the discriminative information is packed into the first $k$ components, reducing the embedding to just those $k$ dimensions before classification should barely hurt accuracy, while making the small-sample covariance-estimation problem of the previous section considerably easier, since it is now a $k$-dimensional problem rather than a $d$-dimensional one. 

To measure this decay in practice, we compute the empirical eigenspectrum of the $\ell_2$-normalised embeddings, pooled across an entire benchmark's training split rather than split by class. Sorting the resulting $d$ eigenvalues from largest to smallest lets us read off, directly, both the variance explained by each individual component and the cumulative variance explained as more components are added.

A steep, fast decay tells us the visual signal is concentrated in a small, active subspace, with the remaining directions being largely redundant; a slow, flat decay instead tells us the signal is spread thinly across most of the ambient space. 

\subsection{PCA Dimension-Reduction Testing}
\label{sec:method:spectral:pca_test}

The analysis above tells us how concentrated the variance is, but not whether that concentration can actually be exploited to help with the small-sample rank deficiency ($n_c < d$). To check this directly, we introduce a test in which we deliberately compress the features before classification and see how the framework performs.

Rather than fitting PCA separately on each target stream, which would break the class-incremental setting by letting the projection change over time, we use a single, fixed projection matrix $\mathbf{P}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}$, pre-trained once on ImageNet-21k and never updated afterwards. 

Comparing performance across these reduced dimensions tells us two things at once: whether performance holds up under compression, which would confirm that the discarded directions really were mostly redundant; and whether the gap between models narrows as $k$ shrinks, which would confirm that the compression is also helping with the small-sample covariance problem, rather than just discarding useful information.
